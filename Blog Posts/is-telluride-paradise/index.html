Is Telluride Paradise?

Paradise, California, was a beautiful mountain town until November 8, 2018. The Camp Fire destroyed more than 18,000 structures, forced at least 52,000 people to evacuate, and killed 85 people. The geography and conditions that helped enable that catastrophe deserve direct comparison to Telluride today.

Telluride is a wildland-urban interface community at the dead end of a box canyon. Box canyon topography can create wind channeling and upslope fire acceleration, producing fire behavior far beyond what people may imagine when they think of a typical wildfire. Fires in this terrain can move faster than evacuation timelines. Colorado State Highway 145, a two-lane paved road, is the only primary paved highway route out of the box canyon. In Paradise, routes that normally took about 25 minutes took several hours, where passable. Paradise had multiple paved roads out. Telluride has one primary paved highway route out.

Homes within one mile of natural areas are in the ember zone. Under extreme conditions, wildfire embers can travel miles from a fire front, igniting homes and structures far ahead of the flames. Telluride is a National Historic Landmark District, with densely packed wooden Victorian structures that pose a serious ember-ignition risk.

Let’s consider population density. Telluride’s official land area is 2.22 square miles, home to approximately 2,417 year-round residents, a baseline density of roughly 1,089 people per square mile. During the Bluegrass Festival, permitted attendance has been reported at roughly 12,000 people per day. Combined with permanent residents, that places roughly 14,000 people in town or Town Park on a single day. For context, Denver’s population density is approximately 4,762 people per square mile. Every in-town evacuation route eventually funnels toward the same two-lane road at the edge of town.

The Town is advancing two major projects in Telluride’s most densely populated neighborhood. The Shandoka/Lot L and Carhenge redevelopments will dramatically increase housing, population, and vehicles at the canyon’s southwest throat. The applicant is seeking dimensional variations for Maximum Site Coverage, Maximum Floor Area per Structure, and Maximum Building Height, describing the approach as a “compact development footprint.” The application states: “The effects of any differences in use or scale have been mitigated through careful planning,” and “The parking layout is intentionally compact.”

Pocket parks. Nature play spaces. Outdoor gathering areas. Meeting after meeting, the same curated phrases fill the room while harder questions go unasked. In the public materials and meetings I have reviewed, wildfire safety has not been substantively addressed. Nor have I seen the San Miguel County Community Wildfire Protection Plan raised in the context of these redevelopments.

As of this writing, I have found no public wildfire egress impact analysis accompanying either proposal.

Telluride has one primary paved highway route out of the box canyon. The Town is engineering a human and vehicular funnel into a single chokepoint: the Mahoney Drive roundabout connecting with Highway 145. Paradise had multiple paved evacuation routes, and 85 people died.

Governor Polis declared a statewide drought emergency on June 4, 2026, activating Phase 3 of Colorado’s Drought Response Plan. April 1, 2026, snowpack in the San Miguel, Animas, and San Juan basins stood at just 9% of its 30-year median. Fire suppression water draws from the same depleted system. The Telluride Fire Protection District covers 350 square miles, served by highly skilled career and volunteer professionals. Unrestrained high-density urban-style development further stresses both our natural and human resources.

Where is the concern for our overextended first responders?

Where is the concern for public safety?

Build densely. Maximize population. Increase traffic. Assume the fire won’t come. Is that the plan?

Do you remember the 2002 Burn Canyon Fire? I do. Driving on Dallas Divide through dense, smoke-filled air, we saw it blowing up: an eerie, deep red glow pulsing against the ridgeline, ominous black clouds boiling upward, split by lightning bolts crackling above the inferno. The wind raged. Breathing was difficult. It was like driving toward the gates of hell. That fire seared its image into my memory.

That red glow on the horizon does not negotiate. It does not wait for an evacuation plan to execute or for a massive multi-level parking garage to empty.

Are you willing to bet your life on it?

—Kate Fedack
