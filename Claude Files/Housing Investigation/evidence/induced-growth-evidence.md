# Induced Growth and Subsidized Workforce Housing in Resort Towns
### Evidence file — Livable Telluride housing-policy investigation
*Compiled 2026-07-31. All figures sourced; see graded source list at end.*

---

## 1. Framing the question

The question is not whether Telluride and San Miguel County have a housing problem — every needs
study says they do. The question is **where "fixing a deficit" ends and "accommodating (or generating)
growth" begins**:

> More visitor beds and commercial square footage → more service jobs → more workers →
> more housing "need" → more subsidized housing → more resident households → more local-serving
> jobs (schools, clinics, groceries, plows) → more workers → more housing "need" …

Two distinct mechanisms get conflated in local debates, and separating them is the analytical key:

1. **Growth accommodation** — subsidized housing built so that *already-approved or future*
   commercial/lodging/luxury-residential development can staff itself. The housing doesn't cause
   the jobs; the development does. But the housing program becomes the enabler that lets the
   growth machine keep running without hitting a labor wall.
2. **True induced growth** — the second-order effect where each newly housed worker household
   itself consumes local services and generates a *fraction* of an additional job. This spiral is
   real but mathematically **convergent** (each round is smaller than the last) so long as one
   household generates less than one additional worker household — which every multiplier estimate
   below says it does.

The genuinely open-ended driver is #1, and — critically — **the "need" methodology itself**: modern
housing-needs analyses (including San Miguel County's own 2025 HNA) define need as a function of
jobs, in-commuters, unfilled positions, and *projected job growth*. Under that definition, need can
never be met in a growing resort economy; the target moves every time the economy grows. Aspen is
the 50-year natural experiment on this point; Whistler is the counter-experiment that capped the
denominator instead.

## 2. Evidence FOR the induced-growth / growth-accommodation critique

### 2a. Aspen/Pitkin County (APCHA): 50 years, ~3,000+ units, and the gap never closed

The largest and oldest resort workforce-housing program in the U.S.:

- **Program scale:** APCHA began in the late 1970s; it now manages **more than 3,100 units**
  (300+ rentals directly managed, 3,000+ deed restrictions), including **1,652 deed-restricted
  ownership units** as of 2021–22 and roughly **1,815 deed-restricted residences inside city
  limits** per the city's own code documents. Roughly **70% of Aspen's full-time-occupied housing
  is deed-restricted** (2023 study cited by ColoradoBiz).
- **Subsidy levels today:** Aspen's 2024 affordable-housing **fee-in-lieu is $408,362 per FTE**
  (Category 2) and **$328,533 per FTE** (Category 4). Critics and officials alike cite
  **~$1 million per unit** to build new subsidized units (Milias, Aspen Times). The planned
  Lumberyard project pencils near that figure.
- **And yet:** After five decades, **Aspen and Snowmass Village still import ~7,500 workers per
  day** (Greater Roaring Fork Regional Housing Study, EPS/RRC 2019). **62% of Pitkin County's
  workforce lives outside the county; 24% travel more than 50 miles** (Pitkin County Vision 2050
  White Paper, 2025, citing Census OnTheMap). Highway 82 average daily traffic is **up 39.7% since
  2001**. The 2019 regional study projected the regional affordable-housing shortfall growing from
  ~4,000 units to **~5,700 units by 2027** — *after* 50 years of APCHA production.
- Aspen's own adopted goal is to house ~**60% of its workforce** locally. Milias's arithmetic
  (Aspen Times, "We're not housing the workforce," 2023): Aspen has ~10,000 jobs; hitting 60%
  would take ~17,143 bedrooms — APCHA would have to **triple its inventory**. The program's
  50-year output is ~5,700–6,500 bedrooms.

**The critiques on the record** (all Aspen Times unless noted):

- Elizabeth Milias ("The Red Ant"): *"Continually developing subsidized housing for anyone and
  everyone who wants to live affordably in Aspen is uncontrolled growth on its face."* She
  documents that only **0.35 workers per bedroom** occupy APCHA's stock and **34% of APCHA
  residents are non-working** (retirees, dependents) — i.e., mitigation housing isn't even housing
  the net-new workers whose jobs triggered it: *"housing mitigation is not going toward housing
  net new workers created through new development."* ("Who lives there?"; "Workforce housing
  dilemma" parts 1–2.)
- Milias, "Let's get honest about growth": Pitkin County has *"a labor shortage, not a housing
  shortage"* — subsidized units get occupied by people who then take (or age out of) the
  better-paying jobs, while *"the lower paying resort service jobs … go unfilled."* Building more
  simply grows the town.
- The Colorado Sun (2018) documented the "**ticking time bomb**": APCHA units filling with
  retirees who no longer work, so each unit's contribution to the *workforce* shrinks over time —
  the program must keep building just to stand still (a treadmill layered on the spiral).
- The **2016 APCHA Policy Study** (Navigate/WSW/Rees — the comprehensive outside review;
  a follow-on regional study came in 2019) found the early theory that subsidized units would be
  a "step" into the free market **"[has] not materialized"**; occupants stay for life, mobility is
  frozen, and the program must expand perpetually. Its peer-program goals matrix lists, verbatim,
  a stated program goal: **"Control growth and job generation to reduce the pressure to provide
  AH"** — an explicit admission by resort housing practitioners that housing supply and growth
  control are two halves of one system, and housing alone cannot close the gap.
- The **2019 EPS/RRC regional study** recommended regionalizing the problem precisely because
  Aspen "generates jobs at a greater rate than affordable housing" — need is produced upstream by
  development, not relieved downstream by units.

### 2b. The spiral is written into the ordinances themselves

Every Colorado resort mitigation code quantifies exactly how many workers each increment of
development creates — and then requires housing for only a fraction of them. **Each approval
therefore adds net-new unhoused workers by design.** The codes are the induced-demand math,
adopted as law:

- **Aspen (§26.470, GMQS):** commercial space generates **4.7 FTEs per 1,000 sq ft** of net
  leasable space in the Commercial Core (3.6 mixed-use, 3.9 service/commercial/industrial, 5.1
  public-office); **0.6 FTEs per lodge bedroom** (0.3 in Lodge Preservation); free-market
  residential generates **0.107 FTEs per 1,000 sq ft**. Mitigation: **65%** for commercial and
  lodging; residential mitigation phased from 15% (2017) upward 3%/yr to **65% by 2034**. Even at
  full 65%, **every project permanently adds 35% of its workforce to the unhoused-worker pool.**
- **Telluride (LUC 3-720):** one employee per **400 sq ft GFA** of commercial (= 2.5 employees
  per 1,000 sq ft); mitigation = employees generated × 400 sq ft × **40%** for commercial and
  hotels, 90% for multifamily/non-hotel accommodations/single-family-duplex. A Telluride
  commercial project thus leaves **60% of its generated workforce unhoused**, by ordinance.
- **San Miguel County (LUC 5-1303 + Appendix E):** residential maintenance employee generation
  ≈ **0.07 FTEs per 1,000 sq ft** plus construction FTEs; sliding mitigation **0% below 2,000 sq
  ft → 90% at 5,001–12,000 sq ft**; the market-affordability-gap fee works out to
  **$277,850–$353,749 per employee** (2021 calc; converted at the county's **1.6 employees per
  unit** factor from the 2005 support study). In 2025 the BOCC upheld a **$278,009** mitigation
  fee on a single new Telluride-area house — the county's own finding that one large home
  generates a third of a million dollars of housing impact.
- **Vail (Town Code 12-23/12-24):** commercial linkage requires housing **20% of employees
  generated** (rates set by nexus study); inclusionary zoning requires **10% of new residential
  GRFA**; the town describes the two tools *combined* as housing **~30% of the workforce generated
  by new development** — i.e., Vail's code accepts that 70% of new workers will be unhoused or
  housed down-valley.
- **Whistler (Employee Housing Service Charge Bylaw, since 1990):** every new development pays per
  employee generated — **CAD $5,908/employee, updated to $10,177/employee** — an explicit,
  legislated price on the worker-generation of each project.
- **Pitkin County's 2025 update (EPS/RRC Employee Generation Study)** shows the generation
  side *accelerating*: new-home construction/operations/maintenance now generates **0.096 FTEs per
  1,000 sq ft — 6.7× the 2004 estimate (0.014)** — and remodels 0.053 FTEs per $1M valuation.
  Homes at residences already account for **3,974 jobs = 14% of all Pitkin County jobs**. The
  county's white paper concludes large homes now act "more like commercial nodes of activity than
  residences."

### 2c. The "need" methodology bakes growth into the deficit — San Miguel County's own numbers

The 2025 **San Miguel County Housing Needs Analysis** (EPS/RRC for SMRHA) is unusually transparent
about what "need" means. Of the headline **1,114 units needed by 2034**:

| Component of "need" | Units | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Overcrowded existing residents | 110 | 9.9% |
| Residents in temporary housing | 72 | 6.5% |
| Housing 50% of current in-commuters (1,417 × 50%) | 520 | 46.7% |
| Workers to fill currently unfilled jobs (205 jobs) | 105 | 9.4% |
| **Projected job growth 2024–2034** | **307** | **27.6%** |
| **Total** | **1,114** | 100% |

Only **~16% of the "deficit" is about sheltering existing resident households**. Roughly **84% is
accommodation** — of commuters who might relocate, of jobs no one has filled, and of jobs that
don't exist yet. This is not a criticism of EPS's craft; it is the standard method. But it means
"meeting the need" is definitionally the same thing as **provisioning the labor force for economic
expansion**. If jobs grow again in 2034, the need regenerates. That is the spiral, stated in the
county's own planning document.

### 2d. Whistler: the peer that named the problem and capped the denominator

Whistler is the only major North American resort that answered the question directly: **rather than
house an ever-growing workforce, it capped growth itself.**

- The **bed-unit cap** dates to Whistler's first OCP (1976 era): a ceiling on total overnight
  capacity (residents + visitors) sized to infrastructure (water, sewer) and community character.
  Initially ~**30,000 bed units → 45,000 (early 1980s) → 52,500 (1989)** → today's OCP recognizes
  **~61,500 approved bed units**, expandable only "with strong community support."
- Employee housing is handled *inside* that logic: the current OCP allows **up to 1,000 additional
  bed units specifically for employee housing** as a bounded carve-out — worker housing has a
  defined ceiling because total growth has a defined ceiling.
- Because build-out is finite, the housing goal could be a **ratio with a fixed denominator**:
  house **≥75% of the workforce locally**. With ~**2,200 employee-restricted units / nearly 7,500
  restricted beds** (WHA, created 1997; EHSC bylaw funding since 1990), Whistler reports **~84% of
  its workforce living locally** — the best resident-worker share of any major destination resort,
  achieved with a *fraction* of Aspen's per-unit subsidy.
- The explicit trade: Whistler accepted a ceiling on visitor capacity (and the revenue growth that
  came with it) so that the worker-housing task would be finite. Aspen kept growth allotments
  flowing and has chased its workforce-housing target for 50 years.
- *Current wrinkle:* BC's 2023–24 provincial housing legislation (SSMUH/Bill 44) forces Whistler
  to accept some housing outside the cap ("Bye, bye, bed cap?" — Pique, 2024), so the model is
  under pressure from above — but from *state-mandated housing*, not from the resort spiral.

## 3. Evidence AGAINST induced growth / the nuance side (steelman)

### 3a. Not building doesn't stop growth — it converts it to in-commuting

- **Big Sky, MT:** essentially no municipal housing program for decades and no incorporated
  government. Result: **~80% of the ~8,000-person workforce commutes** ~50 miles through Gallatin
  Canyon daily (HUD PD&R, Explore Big Sky, Montana Right Now); the canyon's white crosses are the
  local shorthand for the cost. A needs assessment still found a **1,300+ home deficit**. Growth
  arrived on schedule; the workers just sleep in Bozeman and Belgrade.
- **Pitkin/Garfield/Eagle:** Pitkin County construction jobs **fell 16% (2010–2022)** even as
  construction demand boomed — the same jobs **grew 27% in Garfield and 25% in Eagle** counties
  (Vision 2050 White Paper). The jobs were generated by Pitkin County development regardless;
  refusing to house workers only relocated the bedrooms and lengthened the commute.
- **Gateway-town research generally:** Headwaters Economics ("The Amenity Trap") and the USU GNAR
  rural-gentrification/spillover study document small amenity towns "developing large commuter
  sheds" — displacement, not prevention, is the observed alternative to local workforce housing.
- **Commute-distance evidence from our own county:** the 2025 SMC HNA found residents of
  affordable/employee housing commute **4.7–6.3 miles** on average vs **20.6 miles** for
  market-rate-housing workers and **29.8 miles** for out-of-county workers. Whatever one thinks of
  growth, the *location* of worker housing is the difference between a 10-minute and a 60-minute
  VMT/emissions/safety footprint.

### 3b. The housing itself is a weak job generator; luxury development is the strong one

The multiplier table (§4) makes the asymmetry stark: 1,000 sq ft of Aspen commercial space
generates **4.7 FTEs**; 1,000 sq ft of residence generates **~0.1 FTE**. An 800 sq ft deed-restricted
apartment generates on the order of **0.08 FTE** — roughly one job per 12 units. The first-order
job engines are hotels, restaurants, retail, and (increasingly, per Pitkin County) mega-homes —
not the workforce housing. Mick Ireland's long-standing argument sits here: *"When the
super-wealthy prosper, there is a huge surge in demand for Aspen residential real estate and a
consequent jump in employment"* (Colorado Sun, 2018) — demand for labor is generated by the
wealth economy whether or not the workers are housed locally; housing programs decide only whether
those workers live in town or on the highway. On this view APCHA is not a growth machine but a
partially successful *anti-displacement* machine bolted onto a growth machine it doesn't control.

### 3c. The second-order spiral converges (illustrative math)

The "more housing → more residents → more service jobs → more housing" loop is geometric, and
geometric series converge when the ratio is below 1. Using the SMC HNA's own conversion factors
(1.44 jobs/worker, 1.43 workers/household) and a generous assumption that each new resident
household supports **0.3–0.5 local-serving jobs** (schools, health care, retail, government —
consistent with local-serving shares in mountain-county economic profiles):

- House 495 in-commuter households (the HNA's target) → ~150–250 new local-serving jobs
  → ~100–170 additional workers → ~70–120 additional units → ~20–60 more … converging to a total
  **~15–35% overhead** on top of the original units. Bounded, not runaway.
- The *unbounded* term in the series is not resident services — it is **new visitor beds and
  commercial square footage**, each round mitigated at only 20–65%. The spiral runs away only if
  the community keeps approving the upstream generators.

### 3d. Other genuine nuances

- **Retiree drift** (Colorado Sun; Milias): units leak out of the workforce over time — an
  argument that gross unit counts overstate workforce housing, cutting against *both* sides'
  simple narratives.
- **Labor-shortage framing** (Milias): if service jobs go unfilled even where subsidized housing
  exists, the binding constraint may be wages and labor supply, not bedrooms.
- **Regional displacement ethics:** refusing to house workers doesn't reduce regional population
  growth; it exports Telluride's housing burden to Norwood, Rico, Ridgway, and Montrose — with the
  traffic on SMC roads either way.

## 5. How peer programs decided where need ends and growth begins

| Community | Where they drew the line | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Aspen/Pitkin** | Never drew it in housing policy. GMQS caps annual *development allotments* (the upstream generator), housing mitigation set at 65% — both dials, but no fixed end-state. The 2016 policy study's own goals matrix concedes the need to "control growth and job generation to reduce the pressure to provide AH." | 50 years, 3,000+ units, still importing 7,500 workers/day; target (60% of workforce) would require tripling inventory. Need is re-derived from jobs every study cycle, so it regenerates. |
| **Whistler** | Drew it at total capacity: bed-unit cap (52,500 → ~61,500) fixed the denominator; the housing goal became a ratio (≥75% of workforce local) achievable at finite build-out; employee housing gets a bounded 1,000-bed-unit carve-out. | 84% of workforce lives locally — best in class — with a far smaller program than Aspen's. Cap now eroding under provincial mandate, not resort economics. |
| **Breckenridge (Upper Blue)** | Quantified a build-out target: "900 additional workforce housing units … by the time the community reaches full build out"; goal of housing ≥47% of in-town employees; explicitly prioritizes housing *employees who work in the Upper Blue — "not telecommuters, remote workers, or unemployed."* | Need defined relative to build-out rather than open-ended job growth (APCHA Policy Study, App. D). |
| **Vail** | Accepted a partial standard openly: linkage + inclusionary together house ~30% of new-development workforce; the rest is a conscious regional export (down-valley + transit). | Honest about the fraction; depends structurally on Eagle River valley commuters. |
| **Telluride / San Miguel County** | 40%/90% mitigation and a sliding county scale, but no growth cap and a needs methodology in which 84% of "need" is commuter/growth accommodation. The line has not been drawn. | The current posture reproduces Aspen's structure at 1/4 scale. |

**The design lesson:** programs that defined need against a *fixed build-out* (Whistler,
Breckenridge's Upper Blue plan) have an endpoint. Programs that define need against *current jobs
plus projected growth* (Aspen; SMC's 2025 HNA) are structurally endless, because approving the next
hotel or 8,000 sq ft house manufactures the next tranche of need at 0.6 FTE/bedroom or
0.07–0.1 FTE/1,000 sq ft.

## 6. Implications for a town of ~2,500 with a ~8,000-job county economy

Grounding in the 2025 HNA: San Miguel County had **7,985 jobs (2023) / ~8,162 (2024)** worked by
**~5,668 employees** (1.44 jobs each), of whom **~1,417 (25%) commute in**; employers report ~205
unfilled jobs; 44%+ of jobs are tourism-dependent. Town of Telluride population ≈ 2,500.

1. **Name the two deficits separately.** The defensible "deficit" — existing residents who are
   overcrowded or in temporary housing — is **~182 units**. The other **~932 units** of the
   1,114-unit headline are commuter-relocation and growth accommodation. Council can legitimately
   choose to fund some or all of both — but they are different policies, and only the first is
   "fixing" anything. Publishing this split should be the investigation's first ask.
2. **Every approval manufactures need at a knowable rate.** Using the codes' own numbers, a
   10,000 sq ft commercial building in Telluride generates 25 employees and houses 10 of them (40%);
   a 60-room hotel at Aspen's 0.6/bedroom rate generates ~36 FTEs; each 5,000+ sq ft county home
   generates a ~$278k–$354k housing impact. If the town wants the spiral to stop, the dial that
   matters is **upstream approvals and mitigation percentages**, not the size of the housing
   program. At 40% mitigation, each commercial round leaves 60% of its workers as the next
   "deficit."
3. **The second-order spiral is bounded — roughly a 15–35% overhead.** Housing 500 in-commuter
   households would, by the convergence math in §3c, ultimately require perhaps 70–170 further
   units to serve the servers. Real but finite; it should be priced into any build-out plan rather
   than discovered later.
4. **The Aspen path and the Whistler path are both on the table, and they diverge here.** Aspen
   shows that a town can spend 50 years and ~$1M/unit and still import half its workforce if it
   never caps the generator. Whistler shows a resort can hit 84% workforce-local — far above
   anything in Colorado — by fixing total capacity first and sizing the housing program to a
   finite build-out. For Telluride the analogue would be: define build-out (beds, commercial GFA,
   county house-size expectations), compute the workforce at build-out, and size the
   deed-restriction target to that number — then stop treating every jobs uptick as a housing
   emergency.
5. **But refusing to build is not a growth-control strategy.** Big Sky (80% in-commuting) and
   Pitkin's construction-job migration to Garfield/Eagle show demand simply reroutes through
   Norwood, Rico, Ridgway, and Montrose — with SMC residents in deed-restricted units commuting
   4.7–6.3 miles while market-housed workers drive 20–30. If the town under-builds, it should do so
   knowing it is choosing highway VMT and regional displacement, not smaller growth.
6. **Watch the occupancy leak.** Aspen's 0.35 workers/bedroom and 34% non-working occupancy mean
   gross unit counts overstate workforce capacity; Telluride/SMRHA should report
   **workers-per-deed-restricted-bedroom** annually so the program's workforce yield — the number
   that actually offsets in-commuting — is visible.
7. **The honest policy question for council** is not "how many units do we need?" (the HNA answers
   a different question than people think) but: **"At what total size of the visitor economy do we
   intend to stop generating new workers — and are we willing to write that number down, as
   Whistler did in 1989?"** Until some number is written down, every housing dollar is, in part, a
   growth subsidy — and every housing refusal is, in part, a commuting mandate.
