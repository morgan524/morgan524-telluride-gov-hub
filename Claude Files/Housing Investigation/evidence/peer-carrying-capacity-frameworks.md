# How Peer Mountain-Resort Communities Define and Enforce Residential Carrying Capacity

**Research memo — Livable Telluride housing-policy investigation**
Compiled 2026-07-31. Source quality graded inline: [official doc], [news], [academic], [advocacy].

**Scope:** Lake Tahoe (TRPA), Aspen/Pitkin County, Jackson/Teton County WY, Whistler BC, Vail/Eagle County CO, plus Sanibel FL as a classic precedent, a comparison table, a replicable method for Telluride/San Miguel County, and the standard build-out-analysis methodology literature.

**One legal caveat up front (Colorado):** HB23-1255 (2023) prohibits Colorado local governments from capping the number of residential building permits issued per year. It forced Aspen to strip the residential quota out of its Growth Management Quota System in 2024 ([Aspen Times](https://www.aspentimes.com/news/aspen-complies-with-new-state-housing-law-without-compromising-character/) [news]; [Garfield & Hecht summary](https://www.garfieldhecht.com/highlights-of-important-upcoming-changes-to-the-city-of-aspens-land-use-code/) [advocacy — law firm client alert]). Any Telluride/San Miguel County capacity framework must therefore work through **zoned capacity, infrastructure concurrency, mitigation requirements, and incentives — not annual residential permit quotas.** This shapes the "replicability" column throughout.

## 1. Lake Tahoe — Tahoe Regional Planning Agency (TRPA)

The most complete carrying-capacity regime in North America: a bi-state agency with a statutory mandate to define "environmental threshold carrying capacities" and a hard, tradeable cap on every category of development.

### (a) How capacity is defined and measured

- The 1980 amendment to the bi-state Compact (California–Nevada, ratified by Congress) directed TRPA to adopt "environmental threshold carrying capacities," defined as *environmental standards necessary to maintain significant scenic, recreational, educational, scientific or natural values, or public health and safety* ([TRPA 2015 Threshold Evaluation, Ch. 1](https://www.trpa.gov/wp-content/uploads/documents/archive/2/7_Ch1_Introduction_FINAL_9_29_2016.pdf) [official doc]).
- TRPA adopted threshold standards in 1982 for **nine resource areas: water quality, air quality, scenic resources, soil conservation, fish habitat, vegetation, wildlife habitat, noise, and recreation** ([TRPA Threshold Standards and Regional Plan, amended 2019](https://www.trpa.gov/wp-content/uploads/documents/archive/Thresholds_Regional-Plan_Amended_2019_4_24.pdf) [official doc]). Capacity is thus defined **environmentally first**; the development caps are the instrument that keeps cumulative development within the thresholds ("thresholds… indirectly define the capacity of the Region to accommodate additional land development" — same source).
- The regional growth-management system also tracks resource utilization: vehicle miles traveled, impervious land coverage, water demand, and sewage disposal capacity ([TRPA Regional Plan pages](https://www.trpa.gov/regional-plan/) [official doc]).

### (b) The numbers adopted

The 1987 Regional Plan capped total development potential; the 2012 Regional Plan update set the current release schedule. Development can occur only by acquiring a "development right" commodity — **residential allocations + residential units of use (RUUs), tourist accommodation units (TAUs), commercial floor area (CFA)**, plus land-coverage limits per parcel ([TRPA Development Rights](https://www.trpa.gov/development-rights/) [official doc]).

From TRPA's live Cumulative Accounting Dashboard (2023 evaluation) ([laketahoeinfo.org](https://thresholds.laketahoeinfo.org/CumulativeAccounting/Index/2023) [official doc]):

| Commodity | Existing | Cap status |
|---|---|---|
| Residential units | 48,891 (+512 banked) | **92.5% of maximum build-out** |
| Residential allocations (2012 plan) | 2,600 new authorized through 2032; 1,558 released to date | Released at max **130/year**, conditioned on jurisdictions' environmental/permit performance |
| Tourist accommodation units | 11,039 (+1,170 banked) | 88% of maximum; only 342 allocation units remain |
| Commercial floor area | ~6.48M sf (+344,000 sf banked) | 88% of maximum; 529,880 sf remains (incl. 200,000 sf from 2012 plan) |
| Recreation capacity | measured in PAOT ("people at one time") — e.g. 12,400 winter day-use PAOT | ~32% assigned |

- The annual release of the 130 residential allocations to local jurisdictions is **performance-based** — jurisdictions earn allocations by meeting environmental and permit-compliance measures ([TRPA Regional Plan Implementation Committee memo on residential allocations](https://www.trpa.gov/wp-content/uploads/Regional-Plan-Implementation-Committee-Item-No-5-Residential-Allocations.pdf) [official doc]; [TRPA Code Ch. 33, Allocation of Development](https://www.trpa.gov/wp-content/uploads/documents/archive/COCh33.pdf) [official doc]).
- Remaining basin-wide development potential is forecast to build out around 2045 (~3,525 more residential units incl. bonus pools) ([Placer County staff memo, Nov 2025](https://content.govdelivery.com/attachments/CAPLACER/2025/11/13/file_attachments/3460875/06A.pdf) [official doc]).

### (c) Analytical method

1. Define measurable environmental standards (the nine thresholds) with numeric indicators (lake clarity in meters, VMT, coverage % by land-capability class per the Bailey coefficients).
2. Inventory all existing development and all remaining legally buildable parcels ("potential residential units of use").
3. Cap the cumulative total; meter the release (130/yr); require every project to hold a development right.
4. Make rights **transferable with environmental gearing** — transferring development off sensitive land earns up to 5 bonus units per unit retired, steering density to town centers ([TRPA Development Rights](https://www.trpa.gov/development-rights/) [official doc]; [TRPA 2018 development-rights amendments](https://yubanet.com/regional/trpa-approves-tahoe-shoreline-plan-and-development-rights-changes/) [news]).
5. Track everything on a public cumulative-accounting dashboard ([data.trpa.gov](https://data.trpa.gov/regional-plan-tracking/) [official doc]).

### (d) Outcomes and criticisms

- The cap is real and enforced: the basin is at ~92.5% residential build-out and allocations are a scarce, tracked commodity [official doc, dashboard above].
- **Housing-affordability critique:** the same scarcity machine that protected the lake made housing brutally expensive; TRPA itself now runs a housing-amendment program (bonus units for deed-restricted affordable/"achievable" housing at up to 5:1 transfer ratios, 25% density bonus for deed-restricted affordable) — an explicit admission the regime constrained workforce housing ([TRPA housing webinar Q&A, 2023](https://www.trpa.gov/wp-content/uploads/documents/Housing-Webinar-09-2023_QA.pdf) [official doc]; [TRPA allocation explainer](https://www.trpa.gov/part-of-lake-tahoes-protection-is-a-sustainable-allocation-system/) [official doc]).
- Development rights became a private asset class with legal complexity for owners ([Apricity Law explainer](https://www.apricitylegal.com/insights/lake-tahoe-development-rights-trpa) [advocacy — law firm]).
- Environmentalist critique runs the other way: groups like Friends of the West Shore argue the 2012 plan and bonus-unit amendments loosened the cap ([friendswestshore.org](https://www.friendswestshore.org/issues/trpa-regional-plan) [advocacy]).

**Relevance to Telluride:** the only US example where "carrying capacity" is a defined legal term with numeric environmental standards upstream of the development cap. Requires a bi-state compact's authority — not replicable as law in Colorado — but the *accounting architecture* (inventory → cap → metered release → transferable rights → public dashboard) is replicable as policy.

## 2. Aspen / Pitkin County, CO — Growth Management Quota System (GMQS)

The oldest rate-of-growth system in the Rockies (1977), built on a "quality of life" carrying-capacity argument rather than a single environmental threshold — and the case with the most direct legal lessons for Telluride.

### (a) How capacity is defined

- Capacity was defined **socially and infrastructurally**: the 1977 Aspen/Pitkin Growth Management Policy Plan capped community growth at a rate the town's infrastructure and character could absorb, articulated as roughly **2% annual growth** ([Aspen Times, "Infill: Capping growth, but scrapping the competition"](https://www.aspentimes.com/news/infill-capping-growth-but-scrapping-the-competition/) [news]).
- The instrument is an **annual allotment ceiling by use type** (free-market residential units, lodge "pillows," commercial sf, essential public facilities), historically awarded through competitive scoring by the joint city/county Growth Management Commission (same source [news]; [City of Aspen Land Use Code Title 26, Ch. 26.470](https://library.municode.com/co/aspen/codes/municipal_code?nodeId=TIT26LAUSRE_PT400DEPERI_CH26.470GRMAQUSYGM_S26.470.100PLZOCOAP) [official doc]).

### (b) The numbers

- City of Aspen, recent form: **13 free-market residential units, 112 lodging pillows, and 33,300 sf of commercial space per year** ([Aspen P&Z agenda packet, Oct 15 2024](https://d3n9y02raazwpg.cloudfront.net/cityofaspen/ae4809ab-7c29-11ef-9b71-005056a89546-281a6482-8c28-4870-94d1-39c5de6f7e09-1728589860.pdf) [official doc]; [Aspen Times](https://www.aspentimes.com/news/aspen-approves-changes-to-its-land-use-code/) [news]).
- Pitkin County (unincorporated): GMQS Chapter 6 sets annual allotment ceilings by use; growth is additionally throttled by **house-size caps** — a baseline floor area of 5,750 sf, exceedable only by buying Transferable Development Rights (TDRs) at 2,500 sf each from retired rural development rights ([Pitkin County Land Use Code Ch. 6](https://pitkincounty.com/DocumentCenter/View/5858/chapter-06?bidId=) [official doc]; [Pitkin County TDR handout](https://www.pitkincounty.com/DocumentCenter/View/2925/TDR-Handout-PDF) [official doc]).
- Aspen also added **demolition allotments** (limiting tear-down/rebuild churn, initially 6/year) in 2022 ([Aspen Times](https://www.aspentimes.com/news/aspen-electeds-support-limiting-number-of-demolition-allotments-for-residential-development/) [news]).

### (c) How the numbers were derived

- The 2% target came from late-1970s projections of infrastructure capacity (water, sewer, roads, transit) and a political judgment about the pace of community change; allotments were back-calculated as the annual unit count consistent with 2% compounded growth of the then-existing base ([Aspen Times, Infill series](https://www.aspentimes.com/news/infill-capping-growth-but-scrapping-the-competition/) [news]). The precise derivation lives in the 1977 Growth Management Policy Plan (paper-only; not online — a records request to Aspen ComDev would retrieve it).
- The system was periodically re-based through Aspen Area Community Plans (1993, 2012), which recalculated remaining build-out and reset ceilings ([2012 AACP](https://aspen.gov/DocumentCenter/View/358/2012-Aspen-Area-Community-Plan-AACP-PDF?bidId=) [official doc]).

### (d) Outcomes and criticisms

- **It stopped measuring what mattered.** By 2018–20, only 7% of residential, 9% of commercial and 6% of lodging allotments were being used — because growth in Aspen is now demolition/replacement and expansion of existing homes, which the quota never captured ([Aspen Daily News](https://www.aspendailynews.com/news/growth-measures-and-impacts-not-all-captured-under-aspen-s-current-system/article_b37bb904-a62a-11eb-815b-7f617bf3828b.html) [news]).
- **Affordability:** GMQS constrained supply while demand exploded; real-estate professionals openly cite it as a reason Aspen prices are extreme ([Feldman, "Why Aspen Real Estate is So Expensive"](https://garyfeldman.com/blog/an-insiders-guide-to-why-aspen-real-estate-is-so-expensive) [advocacy — realtor]). Aspen's countermeasure was to pair GMQS with the nation's most aggressive affordable-housing mitigation (APCHA, ~3,000 deed-restricted units), funded largely by exactions on the growth that does occur ([APCHA development policy](https://apcha.org/DocumentCenter/View/93/APCHAAffordable-Housing-Development-Policies-and-Procedures-PDF) [official doc]).
- **Legal fate:** HB23-1255 outlawed the residential quota; Aspen's 2024 code amendments removed annual residential allotments while keeping affordable-housing mitigation, demolition limits and commercial/lodging quotas ([Aspen Times](https://www.aspentimes.com/news/aspen-complies-with-new-state-housing-law-without-compromising-character/) [news]).
- Pitkin County's 2023–24 attempts to tighten GMQS drew organized pushback from land-use attorneys ([Aspen Times](https://www.aspentimes.com/news/local/land-use-attorneys-planners-lobby-pitco-to-shelve-proposed-growth-revisions/) [news]).

**Relevance to Telluride:** the direct in-state precedent — and a warning on two fronts: (1) annual residential quotas are now illegal in Colorado; (2) a quota that counts *new units* misses the real growth vector in a built-out resort town (demo/rebuild, house-size inflation, occupancy conversion). Pitkin's **floor-area cap + TDR** approach survives HB23-1255 and is the more transferable instrument.

## 3. Jackson / Teton County, WY — Values-Based Build-Out with Growth Triggers

The best *methodological* model for Telluride: a rigorous parcel-level build-out analysis, a fixed development-potential envelope, and scheduled adaptive-management checkpoints — without an annual quota.

### (a) How capacity is defined

- Capacity is defined by the **Future Land Use Plan itself**: "Potential growth under this Plan is calculated from our values-based Future Land Use Plan; as opposed to the Future Land Use Plan being based on the allocation of a projected amount of growth." Buildout "is not a growth projection, a community defined growth cap, nor the organizing principle" — it is a *numerical audit* that the zoning map matches community values ([Jackson/Teton County Comprehensive Plan, Appendix I: Buildout Numbers and Assumptions](http://jacksontetonplan.com/DocumentCenter/View/660/Appendix-I---Buildout-Numbers-and-Assumptions-PDF) [official doc]).
- Physical context does most of the capping: **~97% of Teton County is federal land or conservation easement**, so all growth fights over ~76,260 private acres ([Western Planner, 2024](https://www.westernplanner.org/2024/2024/11/16/balancing-growth-and-preservation-the-unique-planning-approach-of-teton-county-wy) [news/professional]).

### (b) The numbers (2012 Plan, Appendix I — all [official doc])

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Existing dwelling units (2009) | 9,815 |
| Additional units possible under prior (1994-era) zoning | ±7,800 |
| Additional units possible under the 2012 Plan | **5,420–9,880 (range reflects density-bonus uptake)** |
| Existing nonresidential floor area | 7.92M sf |
| Additional nonresidential under the Plan | 6.2M–8.8M sf (vs ±9.45M under prior zoning) |
| Rural districts (96% of private land) | cut from ~70% of future units (1994 plan) to **<40%**; 2,100–3,500 fewer potential units |
| Town of Jackson + county nodes (4% of private land) | receive >60% of future residential potential |

So the plan's central move: **shrink rural capacity, shift the remainder into town** — i.e., "no more than roughly a doubling of the existing housing stock, ever, and most of it in walkable nodes."

- Growth-rate framing: at the 2000–2008 growth rate of 1.2%/yr, the buildout range represents **50–60 years of growth** (Appendix I [official doc]).
- **Monitoring triggers:** the 2012 plan required a Growth Management Program review at 5% cumulative growth (triggered 2016 → the 2020 plan update); the updated plan checks at **7% growth intervals** against two management targets: **60% of new growth in Complete Neighborhoods** and **65% of the workforce housed locally**. Missing a target triggers an adaptive-management plan amendment ([jacksontetonplan.com Comprehensive Plan page](https://jacksontetonplan.com/270/Comprehensive-Plan) [official doc]; [Teton County Annual Indicator Report 2023](https://tetoncountywy.gov/DocumentCenter/View/25131/MSC2023-0015--Indicator-Report) [official doc]).
- Workforce-housing capacity is *linked* to total capacity through the **Workforce Housing Action Plan** (house 65% of workers locally) and a **residential linkage/nexus study** quantifying employees generated per land use ([Workforce Housing Action Plan](https://www.tetoncountywy.gov/1484/Workforce-Housing-Action-Plan) [official doc]; [2022 Employee Generation & Housing Nexus Study](https://jacksontetonplan.com/326/2020-Employee-Generation-by-Land-Use-Stu) [official doc]).

### (c) Analytical method (fully documented, replicable)

Appendix I discloses every assumption [official doc]:
1. **Existing development inventory** from the county planning database + assessor records (units per parcel; lodging counted as nonresidential; ARUs/basements excluded).
2. **Zoned potential per parcel** = maximum units/floor area allowed by each zoning district minus what exists; condos with short-term-rental capability counted as dwellings by explicit rule.
3. **Plan potential as a range**: low end = base zoning; high end = base + open-space and workforce-housing density bonuses.
4. Results tabulated by 22 subareas (existing, zoned potential, plan-potential range, resulting density) so every district's future is visible.
5. Ratios, not absolutes, drive policy ("will rural districts hold <40% of new units?"), with annual indicator reporting.

### (d) Outcomes and criticisms

- The 2016 trigger worked as designed — growth hit 5%, the review fired, and the plan was updated in 2020 ([jacksontetonplan.com](https://jacksontetonplan.com/270/Comprehensive-Plan) [official doc]).
- Affordability remains dire despite the framework (median 2-bed rent $3,168/mo in 2023); the county's answer is up-zoning *inside* nodes with steep affordability requirements — e.g., Northern South Park: 40% affordable + 30% workforce deed-restricted, max 30% market ([Western Planner](https://www.westernplanner.org/2024/2024/11/16/balancing-growth-and-preservation-the-unique-planning-approach-of-teton-county-wy) [news/professional]).
- Standing critique from the conservation side that character-district zoning still allows too much rural development ([Jackson Hole Conservation Alliance](https://jhalliance.org/community-character/comprehensive-plan/) [advocacy]); from the housing side, that capacity shifted to town has not been built fast enough (same sources).
- APA lists the plan as a national exemplar ([planning.org knowledgebase entry](https://www.planning.org/knowledgebase/resource/7001987/) [official doc]).

**Relevance to Telluride:** the closest structural analog (tiny private-land base inside federal land, town + county joint plan) and the method is published to the assumption level. Nothing in it conflicts with HB23-1255 because there is no permit quota — the cap lives in the zoning map, audited by the buildout table.

## 4. Whistler, BC — The Bed-Unit Cap

The purest single-number carrying capacity in the resort world, denominated in *people housed*, not dwellings — and the only case where the community actually hit the cap and had to decide what to do next.

### (a) How capacity is defined

- A **bed unit** is "a measure of development intended to reflect servicing and facility requirements for one person" — introduced in Whistler's **original 1976 Official Community Plan** to keep growth "consistent with infrastructure capacity, highway capacity and ski lift capacity" ([performance.whistler.ca bed-units monitoring page](https://performance.whistler.ca/community-monitoring/bed-units/) [official doc]).
- Every zoning approval assigns bed units by dwelling type — e.g., single-family home = 6 bed units, hotel room = 2, pension room = 1.5 ([archived RMOW bed-unit explainer](https://www.oocities.org/thetropics/shores/8959/whistler.htm) [advocacy/archival — mirrors old official material]; conversion schedule is in OCP definitions). The cap is thus enforced **at rezoning**, parcel by parcel: nearly all zoned land carries a maximum bed-unit allocation.

### (b) The numbers

- Cap set at **52,500 bed units in 1989** and held there through the 1990s–2000s ([Dave Burch development history](https://daveburch.ca/what-is-whistlers-bed-cap-growth-and-development-history/) [advocacy — realtor, but well-documented]; [archived explainer](https://www.oocities.org/thetropics/shores/8959/whistler.htm)).
- Development grew from 32,085 bed units (1993) to ~55,491 by end-2017 — above the old number because **resident-restricted employee housing was added on top** (same sources).
- The **2020 OCP (adopted June 23, 2020)** recognizes an accommodation capacity limit of **~61,500 bed units, plus up to 1,000 additional** for specific initiatives (notably employee housing) ([RMOW OCP, Ch. 4 Growth Management / Residential Accommodation](https://www.whistler.ca/ocp/residential-accommodation/) [official doc]; [OCP Schedule A](https://pub-rmow.escribemeetings.com/FileStream.ashx?DocumentId=3871) [official doc]). By 2020 Whistler was ~90% of approved build-out ([Pique, "Maxed Out: Does Whistler still have a bed cap?"](https://www.piquenewsmagazine.com/opinion/maxed-out-does-whistler-still-have-a-bed-cap-7183101) [news]).
- Mix at ~2017: ~26,000 visitor beds (45%), 6,430 employee-restricted beds (12%), remainder market residential ([Dave Burch](https://daveburch.ca/what-is-whistlers-bed-cap-growth-and-development-history/) [advocacy]).

### (c) How it was derived

The original number was an **infrastructure-and-experience convergence analysis**: sewer/water plant sizing, Highway 99 capacity, and ski-area comfortable carrying capacity (lifts/terrain) were each converted to "persons accommodated," and the cap set near the lowest common constraint (performance.whistler.ca [official doc] describes the intent; the detailed engineering lives in successive OCP background studies). The "Whistler 2020" sustainability plan and 2020 OCP re-derived the ceiling by adding committed-but-unbuilt zoning to existing stock rather than re-opening the infrastructure math ([whistler.ca OCP](https://www.whistler.ca/ocp/) [official doc]).

### (d) What happened at the cap — and criticisms

- **The cap did not stop employee housing; it was pierced deliberately for it.** Successive councils allowed rezonings above the ceiling almost exclusively for resident-restricted housing (Cheakamus Crossing, WHA projects), pursuing the **Whistler 2020 goal of housing at least 75% of the workforce locally**; ~84% of the workforce was living locally at the 2010s peak ([Pique, "Bed cap still leaves lots of room"](https://www.piquenewsmagazine.com/whistler-news/bed-cap-still-leaves-lots-of-room-2486582) [news]; [Whistler Housing Authority strategic plan coverage](https://www.piquenewsmagazine.com/local-news/whistler-housing-authority-shares-strategic-plan-and-financials-10444854) [news]).
- The Whistler Housing Authority — funded originally by works-and-service charges on commercial development — now manages ~6,500+ resident-restricted beds; WHA's model (employee-generation rates per commercial sf, charged at development) is the enforcement bridge between the cap and workforce housing ([whistler.ca/housing](https://www.whistler.ca/property-housing/housing/) [official doc]).
- **Provincial override, 2024:** BC's Bill 44 (small-scale multi-unit housing) forced Whistler to allow ARUs on all lots, triplexes/fourplexes on single-family lots — bypassing bed-unit accounting on ~3,500 properties; councillors explicitly framed it as the end of the bed cap's integrity ([Pique, "Bye, bye, bed cap?"](https://www.piquenewsmagazine.com/local-news/bye-bye-bed-cap-whistler-gives-first-readings-to-provincial-housing-bill-8754906) [news]; [RMOW provincial-legislation page](https://www.whistler.ca/business-development/land-use-and-development/provincial-legislation/provincial-housing-legislation-changes/) [official doc]; [RMOW council report 24-040](https://pub-rmow.escribemeetings.com/Meeting.aspx?Id=ccbd784f-d7fd-4e4d-9045-98c2a1396eb2&Agenda=Agenda&lang=English&Item=31&Tab=attachments) [official doc]). A 2026 OCP amendment process is underway to reconcile the plan with provincial law ([Engage Whistler](https://engage.whistler.ca/official-community-plan-amendment) [official doc]).
- **Criticisms:** the cap inflated market prices (scarcity by design) while tourist bed churn (Airbnb-era nightly rental of "residential" bed units) eroded the visitor/resident accounting; critics note the cap number itself kept moving up, calling its "hard limit" status into question ([Pique "Maxed Out"](https://www.piquenewsmagazine.com/opinion/maxed-out-does-whistler-still-have-a-bed-cap-7183101) [news]).

**Relevance to Telluride:** the bed-unit is the single most transferable *metric* — it prices infrastructure load in persons, treats a 6,000 sf house and three condos honestly, and gives workforce housing a principled exemption lane (beds for residents count differently than beds for visitors). Both state overrides (BC Bill 44, CO HB23-1255) teach the same lesson: caps survive politically only when paired with a visible workforce-housing delivery machine.

## 5. Vail / Eagle County, CO — Build-Out Reality + Buying Capacity Back (InDEED)

Vail never adopted a formal carrying-capacity ordinance; its "cap" is geography (a 4,000-acre town wedged between I-70 and wilderness, effectively built out). Its significance for Telluride is the response: **treating the existing housing stock as the capacity and purchasing occupancy rights within it.**

### (a) How capacity is defined

- Vail's planning documents treat the town as functionally at build-out; the housing problem is framed as *who occupies the existing units*, since a large share sit empty as second homes. The Vail Land Use Plan and West Vail Master Plan identify only infill/redevelopment sites (e.g., CDOT parcels, Timber Ridge) as remaining capacity ([West Vail Master Plan](https://www.engagevail.com/west-vail-master-plan) [official doc]; [Vail community housing developments](https://www.vail.gov/government/departments/housing/community-housing-developments) [official doc]).
- Eagle County's comprehensive planning includes build-out analysis alongside housing, economic, environmental and infrastructure analyses ([Eagle County adopted long-range plans](https://www.eaglecounty.us/departments___services/community_development/planning/adopted_long_range_plans.php) [official doc]); the operative capacity number today is a **demand-side gap: a projected shortfall of ~6,000 housing units** ([2025 Eagle County Regional Housing Needs Assessment](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/64946109babad916d648148c/t/682364608b5378148959666e/1747149926970/2025+Eagle+County+Regional+Housing+Needs+Assessment.pdf) [official doc]).

### (b) The numbers — Vail InDEED

- **Goal:** 1,000 *additional* deed-restricted homes by 2027 (Vail Housing 2027 Strategic Plan, adopted 2017) ([vail.gov InDEED pages](https://www.vail.gov/government/departments/housing/vailindeed/about-us) [official doc]).
- **Mechanism:** the town pays owners/buyers cash — historically **15–20% of fair market value, creeping to ~21%** — for a permanent deed restriction requiring occupancy by a household with at least one person working 30+ hrs/week in Eagle County. No price cap, no appreciation cap; it is purely an *occupancy* restriction ([vail.gov FAQ](https://www.vail.gov/government/departments/housing/vail-indeed/faq-s) [official doc]; [Vail Daily](https://www.vaildaily.com/news/vail-deed-restriction-purchase-program-has-had-success-so-far-this-year/) [news]).
- **Cost per unit of "capacity":** 2018 average ≈ **$62,000 per deed restriction (~$82/sf, ~$45,000/bedroom)** ([Summit Daily](https://www.summitdaily.com/news/summit-county-officials-take-notes-on-vails-cash-incentives-for-deed-restrictions/) [news]). By 2021: **162 deed restrictions for ~$11M ≈ $67,900/unit** ([HUD USER case study](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/casestudies/study-081121.html) [official doc]; [Denver7](https://www.denver7.com/news/local-news/as-mountain-home-prices-rise-vail-turns-to-deed-restriction-program-to-help-with-affordability) [news]). Cumulative through early 2024: 175 restrictions, ~$2.5–3M remaining in the fund ([Vail Daily, Jan 2024](https://www.vaildaily.com/news/vail-evaluates-community-housing-progress-at-first-meeting-of-2024/) [news]).
- **Versus new construction:** Vail's own new deed-restricted builds run an order of magnitude higher per unit — Residences at Main Vail (72 rental homes) and Timber Ridge redevelopment (302 units) are ~$350k–$600k+/unit municipal costs ([vail.gov community housing developments](https://www.vail.gov/government/departments/housing/community-housing-developments) [official doc]; [Vail Daily on CDOT parcel purchases, $6M+ for land alone](https://www.vaildaily.com/news/vail-approves-over-6-million-in-land-purchases-from-cdot-for-deed-restricted-housing/) [news]). InDEED buys occupancy at roughly **10–20 cents on the new-construction dollar**, with zero added infrastructure load.
- The model spread: **Good Deeds Vail / Eagle County** (county-funded twin) and Summit County's copycat program ([Housing Eagle County](https://www.housingeaglecounty.com/gooddeedsvail) [official doc]; [Summit Daily](https://www.summitdaily.com/news/summit-county-officials-take-notes-on-vails-cash-incentives-for-deed-restrictions/) [news]).

### (c) Analytical method

Not a capacity derivation but a **portfolio target**: Vail counted existing deed-restricted units (~700 in 2017), estimated the workforce needed to operate the resort economy, and set the 1,000-additional-restrictions goal as the share of existing stock that had to be locked to residents to keep a functioning year-round community; progress is reviewed annually against the 2027 plan ([vail.gov, Goal and Review Process](https://www.vail.gov/government/departments/housing/vailindeed/about-us) [official doc]).

### (d) Outcomes and criticisms

- HUD profiled InDEED as a national model for resort markets ([HUD USER](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/casestudies/study-081121.html) [official doc]).
- Criticisms: per-restriction cost rises with the market (the town pays a % of FMV); volume is opportunistic (only 4 restrictions closed in 2023 as prices spiked) so it cannot be the sole tool ([Vail Daily, Jan 2024](https://www.vaildaily.com/news/vail-evaluates-community-housing-progress-at-first-meeting-of-2024/) [news]); occupancy-only restrictions keep units expensive to buy — they protect *workforce occupancy*, not *affordability* ([HUD USER](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/casestudies/study-081121.html) [official doc]).
- County-scale programs missed goals when construction costs surged ([Vail Daily](https://www.vaildaily.com/news/eagle-county-continues-to-add-new-housing-units-but-some-programs-have-missed-their-goals/) [news]).

**Relevance to Telluride:** where new capacity is physically or legally scarce, deed-restriction purchase is the cheapest way to convert existing capacity to resident use — directly relevant to Telluride's box canyon and to San Miguel County's existing stock in Norwood/Rico corridors. (Telluride's own program and SMRHA parallels should be benchmarked against the $62–68k/unit Vail figures.)

## 6. Other strong precedent — Sanibel, FL (the original carrying-capacity plan)

- After incorporating in 1974 specifically to escape county-approved overdevelopment, Sanibel adopted the **1976 Sanibel Plan**, the first US comprehensive plan built explicitly on ecological carrying capacity: the island was mapped into ecological zones, and density assigned per zone by what the ecosystem and **hurricane-evacuation clearance time** could support ([Sanibel Plan, 1976 text](https://annewhistonspirn.com/sharefiles/Sanibel%20Plan-1976.pdf) [official doc]; [Florida Humanities, "Saving Sanibel"](https://floridahumanities.org/blog/saving-sanibel/) [news]).
- Result: a hard build-out ceiling of roughly **8,000–9,000 dwelling units** (build-out projection: 4,700 SF/duplex, 3,136 condo, 700 hotel rooms, 384 timeshares, 80 manufactured homes), which the city still enforces via a dwelling-unit rate-of-growth ordinance ([Santiva Chronicle, "The Plan"](https://santivachronicle.com/news/the-plan/) [news]; [MySanibel real-estate summary of restrictions](https://www.mysanibelrealestate.com/resources/buyer-resources/sanibel-island-development-restrictions) [advocacy]; [U. Tennessee case study](https://trace.tennessee.edu/entities/publication/4f615bef-44ed-4a49-8e33-3218d1cb06cc) [academic]).
- Lesson for Telluride: **evacuation/emergency capacity is a legally durable carrying-capacity basis** (public safety), analogous to Telluride's single-egress box canyon and wildfire evacuation constraints — a Colorado-legal hook that HB23-1255 does not touch (it is a zoning-density rationale, not a permit quota).

## 8. A Method Telluride / San Miguel County Could Use

Synthesizing the five regimes into a Colorado-legal sequence (no residential permit quotas):

**Step 1 — Inventory (the Teton move).** Parcel-level database from the assessor + planning records: existing units, existing floor area, lodging, deed-restricted stock, and STR licenses. Publish the assumptions like Teton's Appendix I (what counts as a unit, how condos/STRs are classified). *Team of 1–2 with GIS; the zoning-map data for /zoning-map/ is already most of the input layer.*

**Step 2 — Zoned capacity / build-out (the standard method).** For every parcel: max units and floor area under current zoning minus existing = remaining potential, expressed as a range (base zoning → with density bonuses). Follow the Maryland guidebook or Prince William County methodology (Section 9). Output the Teton-style table by subarea (Town core, Mountain Village, Lawson Hill, Ilium, Norwood, Rico, unincorporated density districts — noting the county's 1-per-35-acre by-right baseline).

**Step 3 — Convert to persons, not units (the Whistler move).** Assign a bed-unit-style coefficient per dwelling type (e.g., SF home 6, condo 2–4 by bedrooms, hotel room 2, dorm bed 1) so infrastructure math is honest across product types, and so employee housing can be accounted in its own lane.

**Step 4 — Identify binding constraints (the TRPA/Sanibel move).** Test build-out-in-persons against measurable ceilings, each with a numeric standard: water supply/treatment capacity, wastewater plant capacity, Hwy 145 peak-hour and evacuation clearance time (the Sanibel-style public-safety anchor — strongest legal footing in Colorado), school capacity, and any environmental standards (river water quality, wildlife corridors). The *lowest* constraint is the carrying capacity; document it TRPA-style.

**Step 5 — Enforce through zoning + price, not quotas.** Colorado-legal instruments, in rough cost-effectiveness order:
1. **Deed-restriction purchase** (Vail InDEED: ~$62–68k/unit vs $350k+ new build) to convert existing capacity to resident occupancy;
2. **Floor-area baselines + TDRs** (Pitkin: 5,750 sf baseline, 2,500 sf/TDR) to cap mansion inflation and retire rural density into town receiving sites;
3. **Density bonuses only for deed-restricted housing** (TRPA 5:1 gearing, Teton bonus ranges);
4. **Commercial/lodging linkage fees** sized by an employee-generation nexus study (Teton 2022 study; Whistler works-and-service charges) — commercial quotas remain legal in Colorado;
5. **Adaptive triggers** (Teton): publish 2–3 indicators — % workforce housed locally, cumulative growth since baseline, evacuation clearance time — and hard-wire a plan review when a threshold trips (e.g., every 5–7% growth).

**Step 6 — Public ledger (the TRPA dashboard).** A standing public page tracking existing vs remaining capacity and indicator status. This is what turns a one-time study into a governing regime — and it is squarely within Livable Telluride's existing publishing capability.

## 9. Standard Methodology Literature (build-out / development-capacity analysis)

- **APA, *Zoning Practice*, March 2006 — "Buildout Analysis"** — the standard short professional treatment of projecting maximum development under existing regulations ([planning.org](https://www.planning.org/publications/document/9027795/) [official doc]). APA's PAS Reports archive ([planning.org/pas/reports/archive](https://www.planning.org/pas/reports/archive/)) carries related titles (fiscal impact analysis, growth management).
- **Maryland Dept. of Planning, *Estimating Residential Development Capacity: A Guidebook* (2005)** — the most complete free state guidance: data needs, parcel methodology, worked examples; born of the 2003–04 Development Capacity Task Force ([full guidebook PDF](https://planning.maryland.gov/Documents/OurProducts/Archive/72195/mg-Estimating-Development-Capacity.pdf) [official doc]; [4-page summary](https://planning.maryland.gov/Documents/OurProducts/Archive/72195/mg-Estimating-Development-Capacity-summary.pdf) [official doc]; [task force page](https://planning.maryland.gov/Pages/OurWork/GDA/developmentcapacityanalysis.aspx) [official doc]).
- **Prince William County VA, Build-Out Analysis Methodology (2013)** — a clean, replicable county GIS methodology: density × parcelized acreage for residential, FAR × area for nonresidential, with standardized deductions ([methodology PDF](https://eservice.pwcgov.org/planning/documents/BuildOutAnalysis/2013_Methodology.pdf) [official doc]; [program page](https://www.pwcva.gov/department/planning-office/build-out-analysis) [official doc]).
- **WeConservePA, "Build-Out Analysis" guide** — accessible small-community primer ([library.weconservepa.org](https://library.weconservepa.org/guides/42-build-out-analysis) [advocacy — land trust association]).
- **Balsas, "GIS Buildout Analysis and Urban Planning" (2021)** — peer-reviewed method review ([World Scientific](https://www.worldscientific.com/doi/pdf/10.1142/S2345748121500068) [academic]).
- **Jackson/Teton Appendix I** (Section 3 above) doubles as a worked example at exactly Telluride's scale [official doc].
