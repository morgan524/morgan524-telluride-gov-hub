# Housing in the Upper San Miguel: Six Questions, Answered from the Record

**DRAFT for Morgan's review — 2026-07-31.** Every claim traces to the graded
evidence files in `evidence/` and the housing baseline
(`assets/Housing Study/housing-baseline.json`, v0.5.1). Grades: A =
official/adopted; B = professional study with known n; C = news/observation.
Nothing below rests on guesswork.

---

## The short version

The East End's housing debate is usually framed as "how do we build enough?"
The record supports a different framing: **the region's shared systems are
already at or past their design limits, the demand for subsidized housing is
overwhelmingly below 100% AMI while the new supply has been priced at
110–170% AMI, and every new below-market unit carries $400k–$1.2M of public
cost before its share of a nine-figure infrastructure bill.** The honest
policy questions are therefore price, size, and scale questions — not just
unit-count questions. And one number nobody has: how long it takes to
evacuate the box canyon.

---

## 1. What is the carrying capacity of the East End?

**Land (grade A/B):** Of the ~20,250-acre unincorporated East End planning
area, the multifamily-capable class (Residential High/Mixed Use) is only 476
acres and is already built to ~1.7 acres per address. The big acreage
(17,500 acres of Conservation and Residential Low) is 1-unit-per-35-acres
country: a few hundred by-right large lots remain, mostly on constrained
terrain. **There is essentially no by-right multifamily land supply;
multifamily capacity is a policy variable (PUD upzoning or town land), not a
land fact.** [east-end-land-inventory.md]

**Infrastructure (grade A):** the envelopes, ranked by urgency —

| System | Status | Binding |
|---|---|---|
| Regional WWTP | 80% CDPS BOD trigger hit; permit expired 11/2025, admin-continued; 7 quarters in Significant Noncompliance; on-site expansion canceled after pricing **>$133M for +0.2 MGD**; replacement = new 2.67 MGD Ilium plant, Sewer Authority due 7/2028 | **now** |
| In-town intersections | Davis/Colorado NB at LOS F (174 s PM delay, worsening) | now (fixable, ~$1M class) |
| Society Turn + Last Dollar/145 | LOS C → F **on 1.5%/yr background growth alone** by ≤2042; by 2027 with one 240-unit project; fixing one node fails the other | ≤2042 |
| Norwood water | 300 AF Gurley contract ~2/3 committed; exhausted 2036–2042; plant at 92% by 2042 | 2036–2042 |
| Telluride town water | 0.9 MGD peak vs ~2.0 MGD capacity, expandable | not binding |
| Evacuation | box canyon, one road; county AHMP rates wildfire evacuation "a huge concern," MV lists a secondary egress road as an official mitigation action — **and no clearance-time model exists** | unquantified |

**Method (grade A precedent):** Colorado's HB23-1255 outlaws Aspen-style
annual residential quotas, so capacity must live in zoning, infrastructure
thresholds, and incentives. The replicable, Colorado-legal template is
Jackson/Teton's published parcel-level buildout with plan review triggered
at growth increments; Whistler's **bed-unit** (infrastructure load per
person: SF home = 6, hotel room = 2) is the most transferable metric; and
Sanibel, FL grounded a legally durable build-out cap in **hurricane
evacuation clearance time** — the exact analog of the box canyon's single
egress. [peer-carrying-capacity-frameworks.md]

**Answer:** carrying capacity here is not a mystery — it is the minimum of
the envelopes above, and today that minimum is the wastewater plant, with
the two Society Turn–area intersections next. A defensible framework is:
(1) commission an **evacuation clearance-time study** as the gating,
preemption-proof analysis; (2) publish a Teton-style parcel buildout and a
bed-unit ledger; (3) tie approvals to the WWTP/roads/water thresholds the
region's own engineers have already documented.

## 2. What housing actually gets occupied?

The towns keep no systematic vacancy records (itself a governance finding),
but the record yields a three-point demand curve [demand-evidence.md]:

| Price point | Evidence | Demand |
|---|---|---|
| Below-market (VCA, 50–60% AMI caps) | waitlist ~**1,000 households** when capped (2020); a 2023 studio-list reopening rationed to 50 applications in 2 hours | crushing |
| Subsidized ownership (~$350–405k) | 2026 SMRHA lottery: 18 qualified households / 3 units | **6 : 1** |
| ~140% AMI average rents (Voodoo, 2024) | rental lottery: **33 applicants / 27 units**; 5 units still unleased that December; eligibility loosened to fill them | **1.2 : 1** |

After the January 2025 income-based renewals (82 rents up, 35 by >49%),
vacancy hit **49 of 201 town units (~24%)** — and the town's housing
director said on the record the vacancies were **"not because of a lack of
demand."** It is a pricing phenomenon. The structure compounds it: the
cheapest buildings (Shandoka A–E, the 25% multiplier) accept new tenants
only through Tier D, so mid-income households can *enter* the system only
via the newest, most expensive properties — precisely the product that
doesn't fill. [property rent tables, baseline v0.5.1]

**Answer:** the housing that doesn't sit vacant is **smaller units priced at
or below ~100% AMI equivalents**. Demand thins rapidly above ~120% and is
nearly saturated by ~140%. Since no agency measures demand systematically,
**Livable Telluride's survey is positioned to become the region's only
demand instrument** — its core questions should measure willingness-to-apply
at specific rent × size × location combinations drawn from the baseline's
real program parameters, including the trade-off framing the 2024 survey
omitted.

## 3. What does each subsidized unit really cost, by AMI?

Capital subsidy = development cost minus the debt the unit's rent can carry
(30 yr @ 5%, 95% occupancy, $9.5k opex). At real local costs
[subsidy-by-ami.md]:

| Rent tier | Sunnyside cost ($516k/u, free land) | VooDoo cost ($1.26M/u) | Shandoka Lot L proposal ($3.3M/u) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60% AMI | $352k (68%) | $1.09M (87%) | $3.13M (95%) |
| 100% AMI | $145k (28%) | $885k (70%) | $2.93M (89%) |
| 140% AMI | $0 | $678k (54%) | $2.72M (82%) |

Add the ongoing costs the pro-formas omit (peer-benchmarked, grade A):
~$9.5k/unit operating + ~$2.6k admin + $2.4–3.9k reserves per year
(Whistler Housing Authority audited; Shandoka's own pre-capital actuals
match), plus forgone property tax and the booked refinancing cliffs
(VooDoo $23.05M balloon 12/2032; Sunnyside $6.17M by 2040).

**Answer:** the subsidy is never small. Even at top-of-scale rents a
VooDoo-cost unit needs ~$575–678k of public capital; the 60%-AMI units
people actually queue for need ~$1.1M each at that construction cost. This
is also *why* the Town priced new product at 110–170% AMI — the pricing
followed the construction cost, not the demand — and Q2 shows what happened
next. **The comparison that should reframe the program: Vail InDEED buys
permanent deed restrictions on existing units for ~$62–68k — roughly 5–15
cents per workforce-housing dollar of new construction** (constrained by
available inventory, but the first-dollar test every project should face).

## 4. What other costs arrive with more subsidized supply?

Five layers [fiscal-secondary-costs.md]: (1) the regional capital stack —
WWTP replacement (≥$133M lesson; $139.1M town CIP placeholder), **gondola
replacement $120–150M vs $8.2M/yr of 3A revenue with the operating
agreement expiring 12/2027**, the intersection fixes, Norwood's second
water source; (2) portfolio ownership — ~$3.3M/yr steady-state O&M on 251
units plus $600k–1M/yr honest reserves, plus balloon refinancings; the 2025
housing budget is already $13.76M; (3) ≤$1M/yr of tax-base displacement
(bound); (4) administration that never sunsets (~$2–3k/unit/yr at APCHA/WHA
maturity); (5) the service-population overhead — each housed workforce
household generates ~15–35% more service employment needing housing in
turn: 100 "needed" units resolve to ~115–135 at stable service levels.

## 5. Is there a point where subsidized housing drives growth?

Yes — and the region may already be past it on paper. **San Miguel County's
own 2025 HNA shows only ~16% of its 1,114-unit "need" shelters existing
resident households**; the rest accommodates in-commuter relocation,
unfilled jobs, and projected job growth. The spiral is encoded in the
ordinances: Telluride's mitigation rate houses 40% of the 2.5 jobs per
1,000 commercial sq ft it permits — every approval adds net unhoused
workers by design (Aspen, at 65% of 4.7, ran this experiment for 50 years
and still imports 7,500 workers a day). Whistler is the counter-model: it
capped total bed-units, made its housing target finite, and now houses 84%
of its workforce locally. The steelman is real too: Big Sky built nothing
and got 80% in-commuting. [induced-growth-evidence.md]

**Answer:** subsidized housing sized to the *documented existing deficit*
(overcrowded, temporarily housed, displaced, current in-commuters the
region chooses to repatriate) is need-catching. Units beyond that — or
priced to import new higher-income households — are growth accommodation,
and their infrastructure costs (Q4) land on a system already in
noncompliance. **Until the region writes down the size of visitor economy
it intends to serve, every housing dollar is partly a growth subsidy and
every refusal partly a commuting mandate.**

## 6. The questions that were missing

1. **Buy vs build** — $62–68k per InDEED-style restriction vs $575k–$1.1M+
   per new unit. Answered: buying is first-dollar cheaper wherever
   restrictable inventory exists.
2. **Is the region the unit of analysis?** Partly foreclosed: Norwood's
   water is committed by 2036–2042 without a second source, and
   Ridgway/Ouray are thin markets. Montrose + transit remains the one
   scalable regional lever — cost per worker-housed there (incl. transit
   subsidy) belongs in every project comparison.
3. **Year-30 portfolio liability** — now quantified: the town is already a
   $13.76M/yr landlord; WHA's age-tiered reserve schedule shows where that
   goes.
4. **What is success, measurably?** Still undefined by any jurisdiction.
   Candidate metrics: school enrollment, in-commuter share, workforce
   retention, vacancy-by-tier. The survey can baseline several.
5. **The January 2025 natural experiment** — now documented: the region ran
   an accidental price-elasticity test on its own tenants (82 rents up →
   24% vacancy → multiplier retreat). It should be analyzed once, honestly,
   before the next pricing decision.

## Recommendations

1. **Commission an evacuation clearance-time study** for the box canyon at
   resident, build-out, and festival-peak loads — the gating,
   HB23-1255-proof capacity analysis (Sanibel precedent).
2. **Publish the capacity ledger**: Teton-style parcel buildout + bed-unit
   accounting + the WWTP/intersection/water thresholds, updated annually.
3. **Adopt a buy-first test**: no new construction dollar before the
   deed-restriction-purchase alternative is priced for the same households.
4. **Price to the demand curve**: new rental product above ~120% AMI has
   documented near-zero excess demand; stop building it until the sub-100%
   queue clears.
5. **Publish occupancy**: monthly vacancy/waitlist by property, size, and
   tier — the data gap that made this investigation necessary.
6. **Write down the number**: the visitor-economy scale the region intends
   to serve, so "need" has a definition and an end state.
7. **Field the survey as the demand instrument** — willingness-to-apply at
   real rent × size × location combinations, plus the what-would-you-give-up
   trade-offs, anchored to baseline v0.5.1.
