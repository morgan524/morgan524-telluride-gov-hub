When the Town Judges Its Own Projects



-June 30, 2026, by Morgan Smith


Apparently, our little newsletter has made a ripple. Possibly even a wave!

At the June 25 Planning and Zoning meeting in Telluride, Town Attorney Kevin Geiger called out our last newsletter for “misrepresentation.” He said the recent Colorado Supreme Court decision in the “Butcher Creek” PUD case does not apply because Backman Village “is not, and never has been, a PUD. This is just a subdivision.”

I willingly admit when I make a mistake. So here it is: Backman Village should not be called a PUD. There. I said it. Geiger was correct.

But in this particular case, that is a distinction without much, if any, difference.

Backman Village History

Backman Village (the development across from Carhenge; see graphic below) was approved in 1979, and as far as I can tell, Telluride did not yet have a local code for approving PUDs. So, technically, Backman Village is a subdivision, not a PUD.

Backman Village was approved quickly because Telco (the precursor to Telski) reportedly had immediate financial issues and needed approval by June 28, 1979, three days after the initial application to the Town Council. The plan involved several moving parts, including annexation of the Shandoka Lot L area from County land into the Town, followed about six months later by “Phase B” annexation, which included Chair 7 and the southern part of Carhenge as dedicated open space.

To memorialize these agreements, the Town and Telco/Telski entered into the “Contract Regarding Backman Village Subdivision.” That contract notes the land was commercially zoned, including the former Town dump, which became Shandoka Lot L. That contract replaced that zoning with the allowed uses and restrictions shown on the plat and in the “Declaration of Protective Covenants and Restrictions.”

The contract states that all “covenants” are “incorporated as part of this contract.” In plain English, the limitations shown on the plat and in the covenants became part of the contract.  

While Backman Village is not a PUD, it achieved the same effect: taking undeveloped commercial-zoned land and replacing it with a contract between the developer and the Town that establishes what is and is not allowed.


As the Butcher Creek case reminds us, contractual provisions and the amendment process matter when the Town is a party to the contract.



The key point from the Butcher Creek case is not that the word “PUD” is magic. Rather, it’s that a PUD is based on an enforceable legal contract to which the Town is a party. Those contractual rights cannot simply be wiped away by later legislative action, by either the Town itself or by an initiative by the electorate.

Under current plans, the Town seeks to place a PUD within the Backman Village subdivision for both the Shandoka parking garage and the housing/commercial development at Carhenge. The Butcher Creek case teaches that a PUD agreement is legislative action when first approved, but becomes a contractual analysis thereafter.

So the big legal question is. . .

Is consent required by the current Backman Village owners?

At the June 25 meeting, Diane Wolfson, attorney for the Chair 7 Coalition, asserted that the Backman Village contract with the Town requires the consent of all current residents for any amendment. Since the First Amendment to the subdivision Plat filed in 1980 designated Shandoka Lot L (marked as 48-B in the graphic) for parking only and no other purpose, the Town’s development plans for the lot are inconsistent with the 1980 agreement. In her legal opinion, the Town’s current plans require an amendment with the consent of all owners in Backman Village.

She has also expressed that the Plat currently covering the Carhenge area allows only 141 residential units, not 250 as proposed by the Town.

However, Geiger disagreed that any amendment is required to the Backman Village contract, and even if it were required, “there’s nothing in the Telluride Land Use Code, under Colorado law, or in the Backman Village covenants themselves that requires consent from other lot owners for a replat.”

Is his position correct?

We have guidance in the form of a detailed legal memo on this very issue, written by Geiger 18 years ago and referred to by Wolfson at the hearing. The memo addressed whether an amendment to the Backman Village subdivision contract required the current owners’ consent or only the consent of Telco/Telski and the Town.

After reviewing the documents, Geiger concluded:

“Any effective amendment to the Backman Village Covenants would likely require consent from Backman Village landowners in addition to the consent from the Town and Telco (now Telski).”

That’s the opposite of the conclusion he expressed on June 25 at the P&Z meeting. How do we reconcile those two statements? I do not know.

But given the two contradictory conclusions, the issue of whether all Backman Village owners’ consent is required clearly remains an open legal question. A court could ultimately agree with either position, but it is hard to see how this is not a serious legal issue that needs to be addressed fairly and objectively.

The Town’s Alternative Legal Argument

After Wolfson raised the contractual consent issue—citing Geiger’s 2008 memo—he offered another basis for the Town’s position, based on the 2020 Colorado Supreme Court case Forest Hill v. Monument.

That case involved the Town of Monument purchasing a lot in a residential development on County land, subject to private HOA rules that allow only single-family homes. The Town of Monument planned to build a 1,000,000-gallon water tank on one residential lot. The Supreme Court held that the Town did not have to compensate nearby owners for lost property value because private HOA agreements could not prevent the government’s use of property for a public purpose.

But here’s the thing: the Chair 7 Coalition is not making a compensation claim like the owners in Forest Hill.

Rather, they assert that, unlike Forest Hill, the 1979 Backman Village contract, to which the Town is a party, requires the current owner’s consent, which is exactly what Geiger concluded in his 2008 memo.

Why This Matters

There are good arguments on both sides on the merits of this development, and others can debate whether the planned development is appropriate.

My concern here, and the purpose of this commentary, is to raise the issue of what happens when the Town is both the applicant and the decision-maker. That creates an obvious conflict. The Town may spend years developing a project for which it seeks approval (like  Carhenge/Shandoka Lot L), and it is also in a position to decide whether that same project complies with its contractual and legal obligations.

When a private developer applies for zoning changes, the rules matter. They have to follow the code, meet the conditions, obtain the required approvals, and stay within the legal limits set out in the Butcher Creek case.

But when the Town is the applicant, the standard feels different. The Town seems to be saying it can move forward with whatever it wants unless someone sues to stop it. That may be efficient in the Town’s mind, but it raises substantial issues of fairness to those residents within Backman Village.
