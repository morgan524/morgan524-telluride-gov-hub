The Colorado Supreme Court’s “Butcher Creek” Decision

First, I want to thank everyone who came to the Livable Telluride kickoff event at the Elks last Wednesday. More than 50 people attended, which was a great turnout. We had fun with local trivia, good conversations, and many people who clearly care about this community.

The idea behind these newsletters is to take one complicated local issue at a time and explain it in a way that is useful, fair, and understandable. Some issues are hard for daily news outlets to cover in depth because they involve years of history, layers of local government, land-use rules, court cases, and enough acronyms to make a normal person cross-eyed.

Normally, I would not write a report like this. However, as an attorney who has litigated land-use matters, I feel somewhat qualified to analyze a court decision that affects our community.

On Monday morning, my email lit up. People were sending me the Colorado Supreme Court’s decision in Kavanaugh v. Telluride Locals Coalition Petitioners’ Committee.

Here is an attempt to explain what the case was about, why the Colorado Supreme Court ruled the way it did, and why the decision will matter beyond Butcher Creek.

The Butcher Creek PUD

In 1996, a group of property owners, acting through Brighton Properties, LLC, proposed a new development on the north side of Telluride, near the entrance to town before the roundabout and next to the high school parking lot.

The development was called the Butcher Creek Planned Unit Development, or “PUD.” That raises the obvious question: what is a PUD?

A full legal answer involves Colorado’s Planned Unit Development Act, which is found at Colorado Revised Statutes sections 24-67-101 through 24-67-108. But the simpler explanation is this: A PUD is a special kind of land-use agreement between a property owner and a local government, such as a town or county. It sets out what can be built on the property before any construction begins. It determines factors such as housing density, commercial uses, roads, open space, and other limits or requirements.

PUDs are often used for larger or more complicated developments. They can allow more flexibility than normal zoning, but that flexibility usually comes with detailed rules and obligations for the developer that ultimately transfer to the property's buyers when the property is developed. In many ways, a PUD is both a land-use plan and a contract.

For Butcher Creek, the Town of Telluride approved the PUD Agreement in 1996. The Butcher Creek plat (which simply means a diagram of the development) shows areas for future homes, as well as three open-space areas called Lot A, which the dispute centers on.

The large “Lot A” is dedicated as open space as part of the overall PUD development approval process between the developer and the Town.

The Butcher Creek PUD Agreement also included an important provision: any amendment to the agreement required the consent of all owners within the PUD. That kind of requirement is not unusual. When people buy property within a PUD, they often rely on the rules set forth in the PUD Agreement. Those rules can affect the value of their homes, the character of the neighborhood, traffic, open space, and future development.

State law also matters here. Colorado law requires that any proposed change to an existing PUD be consistent with preserving the entire PUD and not substantially harm neighboring property owners or the public interest. In other words, you cannot easily change a major piece of a PUD if doing so would undermine the overall agreement or unfairly affect others both in the PUD itself and right next to the PUD.

The Lot A Housing Proposal

In 2018, Brighton asked the Town of Telluride to consider an amendment to the Butcher Creek PUD Agreement. The goal was to allow housing on the current open space Lot A. The Town rejected the request because Brighton had not obtained the agreement of all current property owners in the PUD. Many of those owners strongly opposed the change.

In 2019, Brighton tried a different path. It proposed a Town initiative that would allow Telluride voters to decide whether Lot A could be developed. That created the central legal question in the case: Could the voters change the PUD through an initiative, even though the PUD Agreement itself required consent from all property owners? The Town said no.

The Town argued that this was an “administrative” issue, not a “legislative” issue. Under Colorado law, initiatives are generally allowed only for legislative matters. That sounds technical, because it is.

Legislative action is the process of making a law. It usually applies broadly to the public. For example, adopting a new zoning code for an entire town would generally be a legislative matter.

An administrative action is different. It applies existing laws or rules to a specific situation. For example, determining whether a particular development application meets existing zoning requirements is often an administrative matter.

In practice, these definitions are as clear as mud. In real life, there is a large, messy middle ground. Land-use decisions often sit squarely in the messy middle. Zoning is often legislative. But applying zoning or amending a specific development agreement can be administrative. That uncertainty is one reason this case mattered.

In order to legally bring an initiative, all parties agree that the subject matter must be “legislative” in nature.  So the question before the Colorado Court was the messy middle of whether the Brighton proposed initiative was “legislative” or “administrative.” 

What the Colorado Supreme Court Decided

On Monday, the Colorado Supreme Court ruled in favor of the Town of Telluride, finding that the proposed initiative is administrative in nature and therefore could not be put before voters.  In plain English, the Court said this: A PUD Agreement is a contract between the parties that is approved as a legislative act of the Town, but is amended through a purely administrative act, based on what the contract itself allows or does not allow just like any other contract.

Here, the Butcher Creek PUD Agreement required consent from all owners before it could be amended. The developer Brighton did not have that consent. The Town therefore rejected the amendment request back in 2018 on the correct basis that as a contractual matter, such consent was required.

Why Should Anyone Outside Butcher Creek Care?

This case matters in ways that may not be immediately obvious. The simple result of the case is that Brighton cannot circumvent the contractual consent requirement by taking the issue directly to voters. Since they lack the owners' consent, they are legally prohibited from seeking such an amendment.

However, this case also establishes a great deal more. 

Many major developments in Telluride, Mountain Village, and San Miguel County involve PUD agreements. These agreements may contain limits on density, open space, building locations, uses, and amendment procedures.

The Supreme Court’s decision strengthens the idea that PUD Agreements should be treated like contracts. That means the exact language of the agreement matters and cannot be overturned by a vote of the electorate.

That means older land-use agreements may deserve much closer attention than many people expected. PUD Agreements from the 1970s to the present still control what can and cannot be amended today.

However, eagle-eyed members of this group will notice that Carhenge is located almost entirely within the original PUD boundary, and that the remaining sections were added shortly thereafter. Those same readers will likely also know that the Town is seeking to build about 250 housing units at this location.

But here’s the catch.

The 1979 PUD agreement limited the total future housing development in Carhenge to 141 units. The attorney for the Chair 7 group wrote to the Town that the Backman Village PUD agreement requires the consent of all owners for any amendments inconsistent with the original 1976 agreement. She asserts the Town cannot make major changes like doubling the allowed housing density at Carhenge without the required consent. To date, the Town has not publicly addressed this issue with the Chair 7 group.

The Supreme Court’s Butcher Creek decision certainly strengthens the Chair 7 group’s argument. If a PUD Agreement is treated as a contract that cannot be amended without substantial compliance with its terms and cannot be amended by a vote of the electorate, the contractual terms must be satisfied for any amendment.

That does not automatically resolve the Backman Village issue, but it does mean the PUD contract language cannot be brushed aside as a minor technicality, and the Town will be forced to address that issue in the near future.

Diamond Ranch/Ridge and the Aldasoro PUD

Backman Village is not the only example where this decision will become relevant. Another involves Diamond Ridge and the 1991 Aldasoro PUD Agreement.

In 2022, the Town and County purchased the 105 acres of Diamond Ridge above the Airport. About 70 of those purchased acres were part of the Aldasoro Sheep Ranch that underwent the PUD process in 1991. The area the Town and County temporarily rezoned was not part of the original Sheep Ranch or the 1991 PUD Agreement but was contractually added to the PUD in 2000.


After the County approved the rezoning of those 35 acres, a lawsuit was filed seeking to overturn the decision on the grounds that it failed to comply with the County’s own master plan, making it “spot zoning.” The Judge in the matter agreed with such claims and, in late 2022, ordered the rezoning overturned.  

In 2024, the court decided that the entire area shown in the diagram above was, in fact, part of the 1991 PUD Agreement, contrary to the County’s claim.

In 2026, the court decided that the 35 acres (rezoned in 2022 to Community Housing) were not part of the PUD. These last two decisions are currently on appeal.

So why might this new case affect this litigation as well?  The answer is that since the Supreme Court reaffirmed the importance of a PUD agreement being a basic contract, the Court of Appeals will have to look at all the contractual language of not only the 1991 PUD agreement, but also the subsequent contracts entered into and recorded with the County regarding the entire property being within the 1991 PUD.

This is what makes the law interesting and keeps lawyers busy. In full disclosure, I am the attorney of record in this continuing case. 

The Bigger Lesson

The larger point is simple: old land-use agreements still matter.

A PUD Agreement is not just a dusty document sitting in a file cabinet. It may control what can be built decades later. It may protect open space. It may limit density. It may require owner consent before major changes are made.

And, as the Supreme Court has now made clear, a PUD Agreement may be treated more like a contract that cannot simply be rewritten by a later vote of public officials or the electorate. That is important for property owners, developers, local governments, and the public.

It is especially important in a place like Telluride, where land is limited, housing needs are real, open space is valuable, and every major land-use decision affects traffic, views, water, public finance, and the character of the region. None of this means that housing cannot be built. It does not mean every PUD is frozen forever. It does not mean every homeowner’s objection has merit.

But it does mean that rules matter. If a PUD Agreement says certain land is open space, or that density is limited, or that all owners must consent before changes are made, those words carry real legal weight.

That may be frustrating for people who want faster decisions. It may also be reassuring for people who relied on those agreements when they bought property or supported earlier development approvals. Either way, the Butcher Creek decision is not just about Butcher Creek.

It is a reminder that many of the most important local decisions are shaped by documents most people have never read, signed years before the current debate began.

And that is exactly why Livable Telluride exists: to help more people understand what is actually being proposed, what rules apply, and why it matters before the decision has already been made.
