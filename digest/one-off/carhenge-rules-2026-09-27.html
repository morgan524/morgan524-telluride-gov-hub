The Town Wrote the Rules, and Apparently, They’re for Everyone Else

by Morgan Smith
September 27, 2026

On September 30, the Town's design proposal for Carhenge goes before HARC (the Historic and Architectural Review Commission).  The Town of Telluride remains the applicant, asking the Town of Telluride to approve its development. [i]

The Town's plan for that parcel now calls for 220 units. The 1979 subdivision agreements that created Backman Village, which the Town signed, allow a maximum of 157.

For three months, Backman Village owners have asked the Town one question: Doesn’t the development application the Town is submitting to itself violate the density limitations set out in the 1979 documents?

Through a Planning & Zoning hearing, a written appeal, and the Town's own legal brief for that appeal, they have received no answer. All we hear is crickets.

Let me say at the outset what this post is not about. It is not about whether Carhenge should be developed or left as is, how tall the buildings should be, or whether Telluride needs more housing, or whether 157 units or 220 is a better idea. Reasonable people disagree on all of that, and those arguments deserve a fair hearing.

But this is about something narrower and, I think, more serious. When a private developer wanted to build on open space in Butcher Creek, the Town wouldn't even process their application without the neighbors' consent, and it defended that position to the Colorado Supreme Court, where it won earlier this year.

Now that the Town is the one asking for approval, the rules seem to have changed. Either the rules bind the Town too, or they were never rules to begin with.

Short history on the contracts

Those who own property in Backman Village are beneficiaries of a 1979 Contract Regarding Backman Village between the Town of Telluride and Telco, the original entity that developed the ski area.  This agreement laid out the “burdens” and “benefits” required of both parties.  But at its core, it is no different from any other contract and must be viewed through that lens.

In June 1979, Telco recorded a Plat (map) with density limitations and protective covenants, and the Town signed a Contract agreeing to honor them all; Telco agreed as well.

The Plat created specific allowed uses and densities for the development.  The Plat identifies the overall density limitations for the two lots at Carhenge:  Lot 34: 131 lodging or multifamily units, or 112 units with 21,000 square feet of commercial. Lot 34-B: 45 units, or 39 units with 7,200 square feet of commercial.

Does the Backman Village contract require the current owners' consent for any changes?  

Since no legal discussion is complete without odd terms, we now confront whether the agreements the Town signed in 1979 “run with the land.” Rest assured, no actual running is required, which is fortunate for most lawyers.

The term asks whether the limits bind only the original signers — the Town and Telco — or attach to the land and bind everyone who buys in later. HOA rules are the familiar example: they run with the land, bind every future owner, and any member can enforce them. Whether a provision runs turns on whether the parties intended it to, judged by their words and their conduct.

So: do the Backman Village density limits run with the land? You are now an expert in real estate law, and to be fair, the answer is not an easy yes or no. That's why we have so many attorneys in the world!

The “Declaration of Protective Covenants, Conditions and Restrictions” states that the “Covenants contained herein shall be . . . amended only with the written consent of Telco and the Town.” Well, that sounds pretty clear that only the original parties can agree to amendments, and no one else.

Ah, but the trouble with old contracts is that what seemed perfectly obvious in 1979 becomes remarkably mysterious once everyone who wrote it has left the room.

The Contract signed by the Town adds more to the story and states that: “The parties understand and agree that all terms and conditions obligating the Town and Telco . . .are specifically enforceable … by the Town, Telco, or either party's assigns that may be aggrieved.”  This provision adds that the contract also applies to “assigns,” which are people who purchase land subject to the express conditions.

The Contract also binds itself to the Plat and the covenants, promising that “this Contract shall insure that such land . . . shall not exceed the constraints of . . . the Protective Covenants, and Plat restrictions.”

Further, the Declaration also states that these restrictions apply to all “heirs, successors and assigns, and shall, as to the owner of each such lot, his heirs, successors and assigns, operate as Covenants running with the land for the benefit of each and all lots in the Subdivision and their respective owners.”

That language sounds a lot more like an intent to run with the land. For many legal scholars, the last section would be definitive of the issue.

One such legal scholar who carefully examined these documents on this issue in 2008 was Town Attorney Kevin Geiger. He concluded the same as above that the Backman Village Covenants: “it is clear that these restrictions are real covenants as pertains to the actual land annexed as part of the 1979 Backman Village Annexation.” He further wrote that:

“Although the Backman Village Covenants appear to state that an amendment can be perfected merely by the written consent of the Town and Telco, because the Covenants run to the benefit of Backman Village landowners, their consent to an amendment to the Covenants would appear to be required.”

That conclusion seems clear and based on a reasonable review of the relevant documents, and the likely intent of the parties. Applied to the Carhenge property, the Plat and the assemblage provision quoted above allow a maximum of 157 residential units, plus some commercial development, under the density limitations of the Town’s Contract.

The Carhenge application that Town submitted to itself  

On September 30, HARC will review the Town's revised application for development at Carhenge, which is split into three separate applications covering eight buildings. One would assume that, because the Town’s Contract limits the total number of units, the application would be consistent with what the Town is permitted to build on these combined lots. 

It is not. 

The plans before HARC call for 220 housing units plus 8,243 square feet of commercial development.  While strong policy arguments may support placing additional housing density in this location, policy cannot override contractual agreements the Town made.  This is especially true when the Town has interpreted its own rules differently when others seek development.

"Rules for thee, and not for me"

The HARC notice includes a Values Statement in all such agendas, stating that the Town is committed to “Consistency – Our review of applications and decisions will be based on the Master Plan, Land Use Code, and the Design Guidelines and Standards for Building in Telluride to provide an outcome that is fair, reasonable, and predictable.”

This is where a technical legal dispute turns into a fairness problem.

In 2018, a developer wanted to build housing on a parcel designated as common open space in the Butcher Creek PUD. But before any application was filed, Geiger sent the developer’s lawyer an unpublished Court of Appeals decision, Wottowa v. Town of Telluride — a case the Town lost, holding that recorded plat restrictions in the PUD “cannot be modified by the unilateral action of Telluride exercising its zoning authority.”

He later testified that although Wottowa was unpublished, “the Town was a direct party in that litigation, so I very much view it as binding on the Town.”

In short, the Town took the position that, without the required written consent of all Butcher Creek owners, it would not even accept the application as complete. No hearings set or allowed.

This position was memorialized in an email that Tom Kennedy, representing some of the concerned Butcher Creek owners, sent to Geiger:

“It was the position of the town that it would not accept or process land use applications from anyone pursuing new development or modifications to existing approvals for … any other property in Butcher Creek without the unanimous approval of the Butcher Creek lot owners.”

The developer tried to circumvent the Town with a ballot measure, and the Town fought that proposed ballot measure in the Colorado Supreme Court and won this year. The Court concluded that amending a PUD agreement is an administrative matter and is not a proper subject for a ballot measure.

In the Town’s trial brief for the Butcher Creek case, the Town argued that agreements like the PUD contract “do not anoint developers as ‘kings’ for life, allowing unfettered discretion to modify the basic terms and development potential for the entire PUD and its residents — all without consulting the residents who actually live there.”

That is the Town of Telluride, in its own words, defending neighbors’ rights four years ago. Needless to say, that statement does not age well for the Town, given its current position on Carhenge.

The question the Town has not answered

If the Town believes the density limitations in its Backman Village contract are not valid, so no consent is required of the current owners as a condition of even applying for a development permit, the Town owes the owners a proper written legal explanation.   This has never been provided.

At a June P&Z meeting, Geiger referred to a provision in the “Declaration of Protective Covenants” that “[n]o lot contained within this Plat shall be further subdivided nor replatted except in accordance with all applicable laws.” At the hearing, Mr. Geiger asserted that “all applicable laws is what you do at Planning and Zoning when you have a replat or a re-subdivision of a piece of property.”

While this statement is true as far as it goes, the provision is not limited to Town laws; it clearly means all laws, which in this case includes contract law.  This does not mean P&Z must make legal decisions on this issue.  Rather, it means the Town owes its citizens the courtesy of handling the issue appropriately and fairly.

What the Town should do

The Town has every right, while wearing the hat of the applicant, to disagree with Geiger’s 2008 legal conclusion and assert a basis for why the Contract does not control. However, wearing the hat of the Town, it has at least a moral and perhaps legal obligation not to act as if the 1979 Contract, to which it is a party, did not exist.

Rather, the Town has two ways to properly deal with this legal dispute: (1) reduce the density of the Carhenge development to that allowed by its Contract, or (2) bring a legal condemnation action against the density provisions only, seeking to have them either deemed inapplicable or having them removed by the court based on a showing of public use.  [ii]

Such an action provides notice to all owners of the claims and the basis of the claims, and a chance to respond. The Court will then be in a position to determine (1) whether the Contract requires consent, and (2) whether fair compensation is due for removing the density limits. 

What the Town should not do is what it is doing: act like a legal contract does not exist. 

Both hearings are on Wednesday, September 30, at Rebekah Hall, 113 W. Columbia. Town Council takes up the appeal of the Carhenge lot line vacation at a special meeting at 10:00 a.m., and HARC considers the Carhenge development application at 5:30 p.m. Written comments go to townclerk@telluride.gov.

[i] Technically, the applicant is Design Workshop, which was hired by the Town to be the applicant, but the effect remains exactly the same.

[ii] When other public entities have faced issues with contractual development restrictions, at least three cases show that the public entity did the correct thing by bringing a condemnation action to resolve the dispute.

The Colorado case is Forest View Co. v. Town of Monument, where the Town brought a condemnation action to remove the private HOA covenants that prevented the building of a million-gallon water tank. 

In Utah, the case of Providence City v. Thompson involved a city seeking to condemn a deed limitation on building roads on property it owned.

In Township of Millburn v. Pitt, a New Jersey case, the Township brought a condemnation action to remove a property limitation that prevented it from building a library on the property.
