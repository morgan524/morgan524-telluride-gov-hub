The Town Wrote the Rules, and Apparently, They’re for Everyone Else

By Morgan Smith · September 27, 2026

On September 30, the Town’s design proposal for Carhenge goes before HARC. The Town of Telluride remains the applicant, asking the Town of Telluride for permission.

Vacating a lot line sounds like a pretty inconsequential act, but it underpins everything that comes after. Erase the line between Carhenge Lots 34 and 34B and you have a single 4.1-acre parcel, and the Town’s own plan for that parcel is 220 units. The 1979 subdivision agreements that created Backman Village, which the Town signed, only allow a maximum of 157.

For three months, Backman Village owners have asked the Town one question: Doesn’t the development application the Town is submitting to itself violate the density limitations set out in the 1979 documents? Not whether the plans are a good or bad idea, but whether the current Town plans are even contractually allowed. Through a P&Z hearing, a written appeal, and the Town’s own legal brief for that appeal, no answer.

All we can hear is crickets.

Let me say at the outset what this newsletter is not about. It is not about whether Carhenge should be developed or left as is, how tall the buildings should be, or whether Telluride needs more housing. Reasonable people disagree on all of that, and those arguments deserve a fair hearing.

This is about something narrower and, I think, more serious. When a private developer wanted to build on open space in Butcher Creek, the Town would not process their application without the neighbors’ consent, and defended that position to the Colorado Supreme Court, where it won this year.

Now that the Town is the one asking for approval, the rules seem to have changed. Either the rules bind the Town too, or they were never rules to begin with.
Short History on the Contracts

Those who own property in Backman Village are beneficiaries of a 1979 Contract Regarding Backman Village Subdivision between the Town of Telluride and Telco, the original entity that developed the ski area. This agreement laid out the “burdens” and “benefits” required of both parties. But at its core, it is no different than any other contract and must be looked at under that lens.

In June 1979, Telco recorded a Plat (map) with density limitations and protective covenants, and the Town signed a Contract agreeing to honor them all.

The Plat created specific allowed uses and densities. For the two lots at Carhenge: Lot 34 — 131 lodging or multifamily units, or 112 units with 21,000 square feet of commercial. Lot 34-B — 45 units, or 39 units with 7,200 square feet of commercial.

The Contract also anticipated the exact maneuver now in front of HARC. It expressly permits Lot 34B to be folded into Lot 34, and it states what becomes of the density when that happens:
“Lot 34B at the option of Telco and the successors in interest to lot 34B may become part of lot 34 as an assemblage thereof. In such event, the permitted densities set forth above for lot 34B shall be added, without reduction or limitation to the densities permitted for lot 34 as shown on sheet 1 of the Plat.”
— Contract Regarding Backman Village Subdivision, June 29, 1979

Added, without reduction or limitation. The parties in 1979 foresaw the merger and wrote down the ceiling that follows it. Erasing the lot line does not erase the limits; it adds them together. That is how two separately capped lots become one lot capped at 157 units alongside the 8,243 square feet of commercial the Town’s own plan includes.
Does the Contract Require Consent of the Current Owners?

Since no legal discussion is complete without odd terms, we now confront whether the agreements the Town signed in 1979 “run with the land.” Rest assured, no actual running is required, which is fortunate for most lawyers.

The term asks whether the limits bind only the original signers — the Town and Telco — or attach to the land and bind everyone who buys in later. HOA rules are the familiar example: they run with the land, bind every future owner, and any member can enforce them. Whether a provision runs turns on whether the parties intended it to, judged by their words and their conduct.

So: do the Backman Village density limits run with the land? You are now an expert in real estate law, and to be entirely fair, the answer is not an easy yes or no. That’s why we have so many attorneys in the world!

The Declaration of Protective Covenants, Conditions and Restrictions states that the “Covenants contained herein shall be . . . amended only with the written consent of Telco and the Town.” Well, that sounds pretty clear that only the original parties can agree to amendments, and no one else.

Ah, but the trouble with old contracts is that what seemed perfectly obvious in 1979 becomes remarkably mysterious once everyone who wrote it has left the room.

The Contract signed by the Town adds more to the story, stating that “all terms and conditions obligating the Town and Telco . . . are specifically enforceable … by the Town, Telco, or either party’s assigns that may be aggrieved.” That provision extends the contract to “assigns” — people who purchase land subject to the express conditions. The Contract also binds itself to the Plat and the covenants, promising that “this Contract shall insure that such land . . . shall not exceed the constraints of . . . the Protective Covenants, and Plat restrictions.”

Further, the Declaration concludes that these restrictions apply to all “heirs, successors and assigns, and shall, as to the owner of each such lot, his heirs, successors and assigns, operate as Covenants running with the land for the benefit of each and all lots in the Subdivision and their respective owners.”

That language sounds a lot more like an intent to run with the land. For many legal scholars, that would be definitive.

One such legal scholar who carefully examined these documents in 2008 was Town Attorney Kevin Geiger. He concluded that “it is clear that these restrictions are real covenants as pertains to the actual land annexed as part of the 1979 Backman Village Annexation.” He further wrote:
“Although the Backman Village Covenants appear to state that an amendment can be perfected merely by the written consent of the Town and Telco, because the Covenants run to the benefit of Backman Village landowners, their consent to an amendment to the Covenants would appear to be required.”
— Town Attorney Kevin Geiger, memorandum of August 29, 2008

Applied to the Carhenge property, the Plat and the assemblage provision quoted above allow a maximum of 157 residential units, plus 8,243 square feet of commercial development, under the density limitations of the Town’s own contract.
The Application the Town Submitted to Itself

On September 30, HARC will review the Town’s revised application for development at Carhenge, split into three separate applications covering eight buildings. One would assume that, because the Town contracted to limit the total number of units, the application would be consistent with what the Town is permitted to build on these combined lots.

It is not.

The plans before HARC call for 220 housing units plus 8,243 square feet of commercial development. While there may be strong policy arguments for additional housing density, policy cannot override contractual agreements made by the Town. This is especially true when the Town has demonstrated the exact opposite interpretation of its own rules when others seek development.

There is a second change worth noticing. When this project came before HARC in the spring, the Town asked for four PUD variations, including relief from the Land Use Code limit of 30,000 square feet per structure. Building D was then a single building of 74,400 square feet, and Building E a single building of 94,200. The September package asks for no variations at all and reports compliance on every dimensional line.

It got there by dividing those two buildings into five. Building D is now D1 at 28,947 square feet and D2 at 29,331. Building E is now E1 at 29,731, E2 at 29,099, and E3 at 25,512. Every one lands just beneath the 30,000-square-foot ceiling — the largest by 269 square feet. The same instinct that split this project into a lot line vacation over here and a building application over there has now been applied to the buildings themselves.

That matters for more than appearances. The Code makes a PUD variation the only route to exceeding these requirements. By designing the PUD away, the Town has removed the one mechanism in its own rules for exceeding anything, which leaves the 1979 density limits standing with nothing in the Land Use Code to set against them.

One more thing about that package. It runs 170 pages. It does not mention the 1979 covenants, the recorded plat restrictions, or the density limits even once.
Rules for Thee, and Not for Me

The HARC notice includes a Values Statement in all such agendas, stating that the Town is committed to “Consistency – Our review of applications and decisions will be based on the Master Plan, Land Use Code, and the Design Guidelines and Standards for Building in Telluride to provide an outcome that is fair, reasonable, and predictable.”

This is where a technical legal dispute turns into a fairness problem.

In 2018, a developer wanted to build housing on a parcel designated as common open space in the Butcher Creek PUD. Before any application was filed, Mr. Geiger sent the developer’s lawyer an unpublished Court of Appeals decision, Wottowa v. Town of Telluride — a case the Town lost, holding that recorded plat restrictions in the PUD “cannot be modified by the unilateral action of Telluride exercising its zoning authority.” He later testified under oath that although Wottowa was unpublished, “the Town was a direct party in that litigation, so I very much view it as binding on the Town.”

In short, the Town took the position that, without the written consent of all owners in Butcher Creek, it would not even accept the application as complete. No hearings set or allowed. That position was memorialized in an email from Tom Kennedy, representing some of the concerned Butcher Creek owners, to Mr. Geiger:
“It was the position of the town that it would not accept or process land use applications from anyone pursuing new development or modifications to existing approvals for … any other property in Butcher Creek without the unanimous approval of the Butcher Creek lot owners.”
— Thomas Kennedy to Town Attorney Kevin Geiger, May 28, 2019

The developer tried to circumvent the Town with a ballot measure, and the Town fought that measure to the Colorado Supreme Court and won this year. The Court concluded that amending a PUD agreement is an administrative act rather than a legislative one, and so is not a proper subject for a ballot measure.

In the Town’s trial brief for the Butcher Creek case, the Town argued that agreements like the PUD contract “do not anoint developers as ‘kings’ for life, allowing unfettered discretion to modify the basic terms and development potential for the entire PUD and its residents — all without consulting the residents who actually live there.”

That is the Town of Telluride, in its own words, defending neighbors’ rights four years ago. Needless to say, that statement does not age well given the Town’s current position on Carhenge.
What the Town Should Do

If the Town believes the density limitations are not valid, and that no consent is required of the current owners, the Town owes the owners a proper written legal explanation. This has never been provided.

The Town has every right, while wearing the hat of the applicant, to disagree with Mr. Geiger’s 2008 conclusion and assert a basis for why the Contract does not control. However, the Town, wearing the hat of the Town, has at least a moral and perhaps legal obligation not to act as if the 1979 Contract, to which it is a party, did not exist.

There are two proper ways to deal with this dispute: (1) reduce the density of the Carhenge development to what the Contract allows, or (2) bring a condemnation action against the density provisions only, seeking to have them deemed inapplicable or removed by a court on a showing of public use.

Such an action gives all owners notice of the claims and a chance to respond. The court would then decide whether the Contract requires consent, and whether fair compensation is due for removing the density limits.

What the Town should not do is exactly what it is doing.

Sending out the crickets.
