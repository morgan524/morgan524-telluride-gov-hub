Shandoka Watch

The story poles that told the story

By Morgan Smith

For months, the proposed redevelopment of the parking area in front of Shandoka existed, as most large public projects do: plans, elevations, presentations, and meeting packets, all readily available. Then the story poles went up, and something changed.

Livable Telluride held an online survey from August 15 to August 18, asking residents about their knowledge of the Shandoka/Lot L proposal, whether viewing the story poles affected their understanding, their concerns, and their opinions on the next steps for the Town.

216 people responded. Of those, 148 took the extra time to leave a written comment. The results don’t tell us what every Telluride resident thinks. This was an open, voluntary survey, not a random scientific sample. The respondents were self-selected, and people with strong opinions are generally more likely to respond. Before taking the survey, 65% of respondents said they were either “very familiar” or “somewhat familiar” with the project. That sounds like a reasonably informed group, but when we asked whether they knew, before the story poles went up, that portions of the project could reach approximately 50 feet in height, only 31% said yes and 64% said no.

Then we asked whether seeing the story poles changed their understanding of the project’s scale: 73% said it changed their understanding significantly or somewhat.

We asked respondents how they felt about the project now, and 81% strongly or somewhat opposed it. People repeatedly asked how the project would be financed; what additional traffic it would add to Mahoney Drive and the west end of town; how many years of construction would be required; about pedestrian safety, parking, the effect on existing Shandoka residents, and whether other locations or smaller projects had been adequately considered.

Several respondents asked to see a construction budget, financing plan, or project pro forma before the Town proceeds. One put it simply: “Show us a pro forma for the TOTAL cost and revenue, and proposed finance plan for the project.”

Others focused on traffic and neighborhood impacts. One respondent wrote that additional cars, congestion, pollution and noise would degrade both residents’ quality of life and Telluride’s character.

Not everyone opposed it

The minority view deserves to be heard too. Some respondents argued that Telluride desperately needs affordable housing and that increasing density at Shandoka is preferable to allowing the community to become dominated by empty second homes. One supporter wrote that the Town needs the housing, daycare, and parking, and the project could help make Telluride more livable as the region grows. Another urged the Town to “Build as much affordable housing in the east end of the county as is humanly possible!”

By the numbers

First, there is no question that the Town of Telluride has a parking issue, and it gets exponentially worse with heavy weekend traffic. Then you look at the existing free parking in Shandoka and Carhenge, which add up to 618 spots, about half of which must be available for skiers during the winter based on prior agreements with Telski.

If you add the 281 new units, with a single parking space allotted to each unit, it leaves just over 200. However, allowing only a single parking space per unit does not mean people in a unit have only one car. Rather, a more typical estimate is about 1.4 cars per unit (TLUC § 3-221.E, Ord. 1523, 2020), which means a net gain of about 100 spots.

Telluride assumes each unit will house an average of 2.1 people, meaning 600 or so new full-time residents would be housed in these units, increasing Telluride’s population from about 2,100 to around 2,700 full-time residents.

So right now, the Town offers 618 spots for free. Assuming all of those spaces remain free, and the spaces for the new units are free, it is hard to imagine how a large underground/above-ground parking structure can be financed when most, if not all, users do not pay for parking.

Town projects are the speeding train without any conductor

At the recent HARC (Historic and Architectural Review Commission) meeting, the Town’s consultants presented their case for approval to a tough crowd on the HARC board. Comments from public speakers were categorically against the development, and the Livable Telluride survey results provided to HARC had the same feedback.

HARC chair Mark Shambaugh stated that “right now we look down a parking lot that’s pretty ugly. So, I think it can’t get much uglier,” and is “ten pounds in a five-pound bag.” Mr. Shambaugh also stated that “without major surgery,” this proposal cannot pass. “The parking garage must be split into at least two buildings, and its height is at least one story too high, if not more.”

Commissioner Matt Lee added that the development is grossly out of scale with the rest of the town, and “I do not even think that if the project were cut in half, it would do it.”

Commissioner Sherri Gourlay stated that “it’s so much bigger than anything contemplated by the guidelines.” Andrew Williamson added that “it’s just overall, my opinion that it is way too big. It seems like they’re trying to do things that benefit the public, but the entire public is against this project.”

All of the above indicates a strong sense that HARC, under the current town land use code, would not, and will not, approve this mass and scale, which is many times above allowances.

When the third-party developer would throw in the towel

Any normal developer concerned about the bottom line would take the message that this project is not going to pass HARC in any similar form, and that, because of the cost of building underground, the finances will not work. The developer would politely abandon the project and try for something entirely new.

But the Town is not a third-party developer; it is the applicant, the judge, and the payor of all consulting costs, whether or not this project is developed. There is no cost-benefit analysis for the paid consultants, who are free to spend more money trying to fit this ten-pound project into a five-pound bag, a task that is highly unlikely to happen.

The necessary remedy

The Town, as the applicant, can ignore the situation and continue paying consulting fees until a body like HARC or P&Z decides to end the project with a public vote.

Alternatively, the town can take responsibility for this project by scheduling a formal vote to either continue or halt the process. This step would enable our elected officials to make a clear decision—either approve continuation or stop now. So far, they haven’t voted to advance beyond a mere “feasibility study,” which was completed in late 2022 during a work session. The project has moved past this initial assessment and now needs proper official approval or denial.

Rather than continuing to pay consultants to redesign something that will remain a seriously unloved project at best, the Town should consider a comprehensive parking supply-and-demand study, as Estes Park, Breckenridge, Steamboat Springs, and other ski towns have done.

If the study concludes that a 900-space garage at this location is still the best option, the concept should be considered. If the study identifies better options, those should be considered as well. The study should include not only ski-season parking but also festival parking for a full picture of all the issues.

To ensure credibility, this study should be fully independent of the usual Town planning officials and the design teams for Shandoka Lot L. The study should also be kept separate from the housing issue, as combining both topics creates unnecessary confusion for decision-making.

Now is the time for the Town Council to take the conductor’s seat and provide direction and guidance before another $1 million is spent on trying to shoe-horn those ten pounds in a five pound bag.
