The Carhenge Story Poles Are Going Up. So Are the Questions.

By LT Staff · July 8, 2026

For all of you who use Carhenge for parking regularly, get ready for a wrinkle in your plans.

The Town of Telluride has announced that the Carhenge Lot will be closed to parking and vehicle access from Monday, July 6, through Friday, July 17, for the installation of story poles as part of the Carhenge Lot Redevelopment Project.

For people who usually park at Carhenge, the Town is directing drivers to other surface lots, including Shandoka and Entrada/Longwill. The Town is also temporarily allowing commuters to park in designated G permit areas without a G permit during the closure, although metered-zone restrictions still apply.

But the real issue is not whether people can find parking for the next two weeks. The real issue is that the Town appears to be moving full steam ahead with the Carhenge project while serious legal and process questions remain unresolved.

That is not a small thing. It is the thing.
Story poles going up at the Carhenge lot, July 2026.

The Town explains that story poles are temporary frameworks used to show the potential height and massing of a proposed building. They allow HARC, Town staff, and community members to see the project’s possible scale in person rather than only on paper.

That part makes sense. Plans can be hard to read. Renderings can be a little too flattering, and story poles are refreshingly blunt. They are basically the project standing up and saying, “Hello, I may soon be this large.”

For a project like Carhenge, that is useful. A drawing in a meeting packet is one thing. A physical outline in the actual neighborhood is another. The Town also says this step is intended to show potential mass and scale as the project moves into architectural design, and that “no decisions have been made about the final development.”
The framed outline of the proposed massing across the lot.

That may be true in the narrow technical sense. But the Town is also closing the lot, installing story poles, preparing for HARC review, and continuing what it describes as necessary steps in securing the Town’s development rights.

“No final decision” may be technically true, but the Town plainly appears to have decided to continue advancing some version of this proposal toward an ultimate final approval. So while the final design may not be decided, the project is plainly moving. This is not exactly a quiet moment of civic reflection beside a pond.
Attend · Wednesday, July 15
HARC will hold a site walk at the Carhenge Lot at 3:00 p.m., followed by its regular meeting at 5:30 p.m. Community members are invited to attend both. The Town says the agenda and meeting details will be posted on the project webpage and meeting portal when available.

People should go, not necessarily to oppose or support the project, but to see it.

There is a difference between reviewing a concept and standing next to the proposed building envelope. Residents should review the poles and ask what the project will look like from the street, how it will relate to the surrounding neighborhood, what will happen to existing parking, how construction might be phased, and what assumptions are being made about access, circulation, height, and massing.

Most importantly, residents should ask why the Town continues to advance the project before the legal and governance issues discussed last week are resolved. If those issues are not a problem, the Town should explain why.

It is also important to be clear about what this criticism is — and what it is not. Questioning the Carhenge process is not the same as opposing housing. Telluride needs serious housing solutions. It needs better use of public land. It needs projects that help people live here rather than merely visit, invest, or admire the view from a heated driveway.

The current Backman Village subdivision plan appears to allow up to 141 units at Carhenge, even before any amendment to the plan is considered. The proposed 225 units at Carhenge, plus 60 units at Lot L, would bring the total to 285 new units. Using the Town’s own general assumptions, that could mean roughly 515 new residents in a town of about 2,400 people.

That does not make the project wrong. But it does make the scale important.

The cost should also be part of the public discussion before the project gains too much momentum. At an average of 725 square feet per unit, 225 units would amount to roughly 163,000 square feet of construction. At about $1,000 per square foot — the reported cost of VooDoo — that suggests a project cost in the range of $163 million. Will the anticipated rents cover such debts with large balloon payments, or will annual subsidies from the Town be required?

Those are not hostile questions. They are normal questions. They are the kind of questions a community should ask when a public body is developing public land and then reviewing its own work.

Maybe this is precisely the number of new units needed in Town.

The story poles are going up. So should the public’s attention.
