Rocket Science Not Required: Making Sense of School District Measure 5A

By Morgan Smith - October 21, 2026

I readily admit there are days when I wonder why I spend time writing these newsletters, and today is one of them!

I’m going to dive into the debate over Telluride School District Ballot Measure 5A, even though my brother-in-law, David Lavender, serves on the school board and strongly supports the measure, while other good friends are adamantly on the other side.

Not only was I reluctant to jump into this debate for those personal reasons, but it also involves a lot of math! To help, I created several charts so you can get the gist even if you don’t want to read every word.

So let’s take a deep dive into the fascinating world of school funding!!

How is the School District’s funding calculated?

To answer this question, you might need to be a rocket scientist with plenty of time on your hands—which is well above my pay grade—so I'll stay at the 30,000-foot level. Luckily, the complex formulas aren't necessary, and the concepts aren't complicated.

First rule: the State sets the per-student amount each school district receives through local and state funding. Every district starts with the same statewide base, but the State adjusts it for each district’s size, cost of living, and student needs, so the amount varies by district. A local district can add to that funding only through voter-approved property tax mill levy overrides, and only up to a limit the State sets as a percentage of that amount. Colorado calls this amount “Total Program Funding”, which I’ll abbreviate as “TPF”. As we'll discuss, this is a poorly named term because it is not the total funding per student, no matter how you count.

Second rule: Colorado only pays the difference between what local property taxes raise and the district’s TPF amount. This means the State may pay up to 97% of TPF in a less financially fortunate district, but in a wealthy district like Telluride, the State currently pays only about 10% of the TPF and will likely pay nothing in future years.

To illustrate how it works, let’s use a glass of water to represent the Total Program Funding (TPF), with the past amounts shown in 2026 dollars, not actual. 

Chart 1 (All in 2026 Dollars)

This chart shows four school years in today’s dollars, so it removes inflation. Once you do that, Total Program Funding is slightly less today ($13.4 million) than it was before COVID ($13.6 million in 2019-20). The 2020-21 dip was a State budget cut during the pandemic, which the State later restored.

But as anyone reading this knows, property values skyrocketed during this period. District-wide assessed values nearly doubled, from about $843 million in 2019-20 to about $1.6 billion today, and many individual tax bills increased even more. So everyone paid a lot more toward the existing mill levies, even after accounting for inflation.

But as you can see, that big jump in what property owners paid did not increase the school district’s TPF in 2026 dollars.

But what did change is who fills the glass.

Before COVID, local taxes and the State each paid about half of Total Program Funding: 51% local and 49% State. Today, local taxes cover 90%. In today’s dollars, local taxpayers pay about $5 million more a year to fill nearly the same gap, while the State pays about $5 million less. So yes, the local share has gone way up because property values have risen, and the State’s share has shrunk.

When school board members say they get no benefit from rising property values or higher mill levy payments, they are entirely correct. But when opponents point out that their mill levy taxes have doubled, they are also correct.

In short, the only winner here is the State of Colorado, which has greatly reduced its TPF payments to the district.

What’s in the school district’s budget today?

Before looking at trends, it helps to know what each number people quote includes. Opponents argue the district is being unfair by making statements like “State funding limits [the district] to $13,591 per pupil.” That figure is the district’s own estimate of its per-pupil TPF without 5A. They have a point: TPF is only one layer of the district’s funding, and focusing only on the $13,591 makes it sound like Telluride’s kids are attending school in a one-room schoolhouse with a pot-bellied stove, with Pa reading stories to them.

The chart below shows this year’s TPF (before reductions under the new formula), plus the other money from all sources that goes into the general fund per student.

Chart 2

This includes the existing 30% mill levy override "MLO", meaning that for every dollar allowed in the TPF, the district collects an additional 30% through the override. With other revenue, such as grants, parent fees, and state and federal funds, the district’s general fund will receive about $28k per student this year.

Is this the total funding for students?

The short answer is no. The numbers above cover only the general fund, which pays for day-to-day operations. Of that general fund money, about $22,000 per student comes from local property taxes (all the mill levies and overrides).

However, the district’s budget also includes separate funds for food service, student activities, transportation, employee housing, the Palm Theatre, bond repayment, and construction. None of those are included in the above number, and opponents often suggest that some or all of such additional funds should be included.

If you add all those separate funds to the general fund, the total is around $48.7k per student, far above the “Total Program Funding” explained above.

Chart 3

That higher number includes construction and bond payments for school buildings and new teacher housing, which will last for many years and serve future students and teachers. About half of this year’s bond payments actually cover older school bonds refinanced in 2021. People usually don't include this kind of capital expense when they talk about spending per student.

If you exclude construction and bond payments but include food service, student activities (paid by parents), transportation, employee housing, and Palm Theatre funds, the total is $31,458 per student.

If you believe general fund spending is the correct measure, it’s back to general fund spending of $27,453.

You can judge which number you think is fair, but it doesn't really change the 5A analysis much. The above, however, explains all the competing numbers one might see in this debate.

How we got here: spending from 2019 to today

For those of you still awake and following the numbers, you may reasonably point out that, no matter how you count, the school's population is declining, and the current overall budget doesn't reflect that.

Chart 4 below shows general fund spending per student in both actual and inflation-adjusted dollars. The continuing increase in the per-student rate is clear: up about 34% since 2019-20 even after inflation is removed.

Chart 4

So why is per-student funding rising even as enrollment declines? 

Counterintuitively, this per-student increase is driven by declining enrollment. Under the old 1994 school finance formula, the State funded a district on the best of its last five years: the current year’s count, or the average of the current year with one, two, three, or four prior years, whichever was highest. When enrollment is growing, the current year is usually the highest, so the district is funded on its actual count.

When enrollment is declining, the reverse is true. Starting next year, the State moves to a weighted three-year average, explained below.

Chart 5 below shows how many more students the State has been funding than the district enrolls. While enrollment grew through 2018-19, the district was funded on its actual count. Since 2019-20, as enrollment fell, the best-of-five-years rule has funded it for more students than it enrolls, about 72 more this year.

Chart 5

So it is entirely fair to point out that funding is still increasing, while enrollment declines. That sure seems like a school district that is not tightening its belt as it should.

During this “honeymoon” period, the district received more funding than a straight count of current students would have provided. 

How does Telluride compare with other districts?

But looking only at current general fund spending doesn't show whether this level of funding is appropriate or wildly inflated.

Chart 6 below shows other comparable ski-town districts and their increases in general fund spending per student over the last few years, in 2026 dollars. Telluride had the largest increase among the peer districts. The main reason is that Telluride’s enrollment decreased about twice as fast as its peers’ (about 8% versus about 4% from 2021-22 to 2024-25), so similar dollars were spread over fewer students. Every Colorado district used the same best-of-five-years averaging for past calculations, but it cushioned Telluride’s revenue more because its enrollment decline was steeper.

Chart 6

So yes, Telluride has seen a big increase in per-student spending, largely because it lost students faster than its peers and the best-of-five-years averaging cushioned the loss.

The overall budget

Now look at the whole operating budget instead of the per-student number: the general fund plus food service, activities, transportation, housing, and the Palm Theatre, but not bonds or construction. Chart 7 shows it in 2026 dollars, alongside enrollment.

Chart 7 (All in 2026 Dollars)

While per-student spending, as shown in Chart 4, has continued to climb, the overall budget grew about 13% after inflation through 2022-23 and has been roughly flat since, with a slight decline in the last two years.

However, during the same period, the student population has declined from 885 to a projected 715 this year. Opponents point to these numbers and claim the school is doing too little to cut overall spending, even as enrollment declines.

The district responds that it has been cutting costs. The ballot notice says, “The District has reduced staffing and overhead in response to declining enrollment. Over the last five years, staffing has been reduced by 8%, which is 12.5 full-time positions.”

While it’s fair to argue whether the school has addressed declining enrollment to a satisfying degree, much of this is due to the “honeymoon” effect of the old averaging rule, which temporarily props up allowed funding.

However, this honeymoon is ending.

What’s coming: the new formula

For those still with me on this mathlete of a post, here's what will happen under the new law.

The old formula benefited the school more because it funded the district based on the best of its last five years of enrollment, which cushioned the decline in school population. Under the new rules, the State will use a weighted three-year average, called a “smoothing factor”: 50% of the current year’s count, 30% of last year’s, and 20% of the year before. If certain economic conditions are not met, it will instead use the higher of the current year’s count or 97% of the prior year’s. Either way, the count tracks actual enrollment much more closely than the prior method. 

The new formula will reduce both TPF and the amount the district can collect. Even if property values double again next year, the school can’t collect any more through the mill levy or overrides under current law, at least through 2029-30.

Chart 8 compares this year’s general fund per-student amount under the old and the new formula, fully phased in, using today’s enrollment and today’s dollars. TPF drops from about $18,700 to $13,800 per student, and the existing override, set at 30% of TPF, falls from about $5,600 to $4,150. Based on the formula alone, general fund revenue falls from $27,878 to $21,553 per student without 5A, about 23% less. With 5A, it would be $24,321 per student, about 13% less. Keep in mind this reflects the formula change alone. 

Chart 8

This chart shows the cuts “fully phased in,” meaning they happen over several years, not immediately.

Chart 9 compares the district’s revenue with its projected spending each year, in 2026 dollars. Without 5A, the district runs a deficit every year, growing from about $0.6 million this year to about $2.6 million by 2032-33. This totals about $15.2 million over seven years.

Chart 9 (All in 2026 Dollars)

 

5A doesn’t affect this school year, since the first collections begin in 2027-28. With 5A, the district has a small surplus of about $0.6 million in 2027-28, followed by small deficits of $0.1 to $0.9 million per year, or about $2.3 million short over the seven years.

A declining school population

Everyone can agree that enrollment has declined and will keep declining, from a projected 715 this year to about 620 by 2032-33 in the district’s projection. As Chart 9 shows, the district’s revenue without 5A falls faster than its planned spending over that period.

There is no choice here. The district must bring costs in line with less revenue and fewer students. Some opponents have suggested drastic steps, like selling off the elementary school or eliminating the Dual Immersion program. Those seem premature given today’s budget, but as the gap grows, everything may eventually be on the table.

Property owners shouldn’t expect declining enrollment to lower their school taxes much, either. The formula tax rate is set by law, and the district plans to keep any local tax collected above the formula amount starting in 2030-31 instead of lowering the rate.

For those concerned that the district hasn't already done enough to demonstrate real belt-tightening, that will come regardless of 5A. If current trends continue, the once-unthinkable options may be on the table later, but they don’t need to be considered now. 

If 5A does not pass, how difficult will it be for the district?

This school year isn’t affected by 5A either way. Next year is when the change starts to bite: the old best-of-five-years averaging ends, and without 5A the district’s yearly gap grows to about $2 million. If 5A passes, the district has a small surplus in 2027-28 and then small deficits of up to about $0.9 million a year. If 5A fails, the district faces deficits of about $2 to $2.6 million a year even after its planned cuts, and the question becomes what to cut.

The mailer below from the Save Our Schools (S.O.S.) campaign claims that without 5A, the school will be “gutted.” That sounds a bit extreme given the numbers above. But while “gutted” is hyperbole, deficits of $2 to $2.6 million a year are nothing to shrug off.

 

Core classes are the most protected. In its ballot statement, the district says the cuts would likely hit Advanced Placement classes, the Dual Immersion program, parts of career education, the Miner School, arts classes, academic and behavioral supports, and sports teams and Ski PE. Those are the things that make Telluride schools special for students—which parents and teachers argue are essential.

Part of this question’s importance for voters is unknowable: it is not about differences in possible budgets, but the unquantifiable value of a strong public school with a 97.9% four-year graduation rate versus 85.6% statewide and test scores well above the state average.

If 5A does not pass, will that unquantifiable value start to diminish over the next few years? Maybe. If 5A passes, will that value still diminish simply because enrollment is declining? Maybe.

The elephant in the room

There’s no question that the timing of this mill levy override is driving much of the opposition. Many people have seen their property taxes double or more since before the pandemic, all for living in the same house.

Many are no longer in a “giving” mood, and everyone wants to ensure any new funds are essential and will not be wasted; they also feel tapped out by recent mill levy increases from other local districts, like the fire and hospital districts.

However, a strong, vibrant school district that sends more graduates to college on average also has an unknowable value, often determined only when it is lost.

There is also the question of whether a strong school raises property values. Research generally says yes. Studies have found that buyers are willing to pay more for similar homes zoned to schools with higher test scores. However, many Telluride buyers are second-home owners, so it is unclear how this applies here.

Conclusion

I hope this very long newsletter has provided objective information for those interested in learning more about this ballot measure. The financing is certainly complicated. I have been as careful as I can to avoid taking sides and to present each side’s arguments fairly.

But ultimately, I hope everyone has the objective information they need to make up their own minds and vote the way they feel is right on this important ballot measure.
