The Gondola and the Great Money Question  

-Morgan Smith, Sept. 10, 2026

The Telluride Times just ran a strong article on the issues surrounding the gondola replacement that is worth a read.

As with many things, the process, not the goal, is the problem. I imagine most everyone would love a replacement gondola if someone else has to pay to build and run it. The love of the gondola, however, can start falling apart when it is time for everyone to pay the bills.

A Brief History of the Gondola

The gondola was built in the mid-1990s under a set of agreements among Telski, the Town of Mountain Village, San Miguel County, and the entities that preceded today’s Telluride Mountain Village Owners Association (TMVOA).

The gondola opened in late 1996. Construction was financed in 1995 through the Telluride Gondola Transit Company Revenue Bonds, Series 1995, with Mountain Village’s real estate transfer assessment (the RETA) pledged against the debt.

The promise itself came out of a lawsuit (Case No. 97-CV-133 in the District Court for San Miguel County) settled in 1999 by the First Amended and Restated Gondola Operating Agreement. That document says the gondola runs free, at least 16 hours a day and not less than 4,400 scheduled hours a year, paid for by the RETA plus 1 percent of Telski’s gross revenue from daily lift ticket sales.

The settlement agreement ends on December 31, 2027, when those entities no longer have any obligation to pay for operating the gondola. SMART (the San Miguel Authority for Regional Transportation) assumes operations and maintenance responsibility in January 2028.

The Next Step

In 2015, the manufacturer Doppelmayr delivered an engineering study of the existing machine. On the question of how long it could last, the study said:

“The past and continued maintenance, along with the capital replacements and upgrades of the Mountain Village Gondola system, could allow the system to run indefinitely. Note that the routine and preventative maintenance on the Gondola has been at a level of excellent.”

This conclusion was echoed in a second report in 2016 by Outdoor Engineers that concluded the Doppelmayr statement regarding an “indefinite” life was “a fair statement, assuming the level of maintenance and upgrades is kept at such a high level as up to date.”

In 2016, the gondola committee (whose name has changed over time) was created to look to the future — which is basically now — and determine what should be done with the gondola before the funding expires.

In 2021, the committee gained traction, summarized discussions from the past few years, and provided four options, as shown below.

As you can see, the consultants did not recommend minor repairs or major repairs that keep the stations the same. However, the major upgrade and the full replacement were both possible recommendations.

In 2022, the gondola committee began planning the gondola’s future by gathering information and making decisions later that year. The community survey showed that people liked the gondola for its convenience and because it was free.

The consultants indicated that a completely rebuilt gondola was preferable to major upgrades to the current system, because “the system has come into age in its fundamental parts.”

In June 2022, the committee—made up of local public officials and stakeholders—decided that a new gondola system was needed rather than major upgrades to the current system, and the process then moved into funding. The consultants addressed how the system would be funded, including operating costs of about $5 million a year as well as the recommended full gondola replacement, estimated at the time to be in the range of $90 million, not including new gondola stations.

The Campaign

Beginning in 2023, the Town of Telluride, the Town of Mountain Village, TMVOA, San Miguel County, Telski, and SMART funded a shared gondola project development budget through an intergovernmental agreement. That shared budget started at $323,400.81 in 2023 and reached roughly $957,000 in 2025.

The partners hired an election consultant to develop a comprehensive campaign plan, analyzing polling data, identifying strengths and weaknesses, and “crafting a narrative that tells the story of SMART, the gondola, and its impact on people’s lives.” The consultant recognized that a campaign narrowly centered on shifting operating costs from TMVOA to the rest of the towns’ and county’s citizens was likely to fail if presented that way.

Instead, the consultants concluded that voters would respond better to a vision highlighting the aspirational potential of a new gondola system — presenting Measure 3A as a “savings account” for future improvements.

This became Ballot Measure 3A on the November 2024 ballot. The same election consultant created the campaign committee “Friends of the Gondola — Yes on 3A” on September 11, 2024.

In short, 3A imposed a variety of fees and taxes totaling about $8 million a year, starting immediately if passed. Because SMART’s obligation to pay for operations and maintenance does not begin until January 1, 2028, the funds would build a capital reserve of around $15 million available at the end of 2027. 

The itemized contributions in the Secretary of State filings in the trial record show only a handful of donors: Fort Partners ($60,000), TMVOA ($60,000), and Shaw Construction ($10,000).

In November, the ballot measure passed on the strength of the yes vote in Mountain Village but failed in the other towns and in the county. The number of yes votes was sufficient to carry the day.

Following the passage of Measure 3A, there are funds to pay for the continued operation of the gondola in its present form, but only a small fraction of what it would cost to replace it.

How Much Will a New Gondola System Cost?

This is where things start becoming an issue for discussion.

The 2022 estimate of $90 million was far below the actual replacement cost even then, and it did not include new gondola stations.

At the June 2026 meeting, the committee worked through a funding scenario built on a $140 million total project cost, with $70 million assumed to come from a federal Department of Transportation grant and the remaining $70 million from local funds and SMART. However, $140 million is listed in the staff memo under “Assumptions,” and Mountain Village’s SMART board chair said on the record that it is “an example, just to show how the numbers flow.”

SMART’s own published estimate is higher. Its project FAQ puts the planning-level cost of replacing the core gondola system at $135 million to $187 million in 2031 dollars. That range does include like-for-like replacement of all terminals, level boarding and ADA-compliant loading areas, and maintenance and storage facilities. What it excludes are the station-area work the towns have separately planned — enhanced terminal buildings, restrooms, public art and landscaping, which SMART states are “not reflected in the core project cost estimate.”

Those costs are additional and unquantified.

The Federal Money

SMART is candid that federal funding for a gondola would be a first. Its project FAQ states that the project is exploring the Federal Transit Administration’s Capital Investment Grant program, “which could fund up to 50% of eligible costs,” and describes SMART as “the first gondola project to pursue this program.”

Buy America

Federal transit funds come with Buy America requirements, and which requirement applies depends on how the FTA classifies the machine.

If a gondola is treated as rolling stock — a transit vehicle, like a bus or a railcar — the rule is at least 70 percent domestic content. If its towers, haul rope, grips, drive terminals, and station machinery are treated instead as steel, iron, and manufactured products, the requirement is 100 percent domestic.

That classification has not been decided. SMART is in a position to argue the legal question of whether it may qualify. The June 15, 2026 meeting packet states that the project team “has received component sourcing information from vendors and is calculating percentages to best make the case to FTA legal for a rolling stock Build America, Buy America project interpretation.” In other words, the more forgiving 70 percent standard is a position being argued, not a rule that applies.

The only two gondola builders are both European. SMART asked whether they could comply. SMART’s Executive Director, Amber Blake, said their responses were too vague to assess, prompting SMART to seek more detail. She put the stakes bluntly:

“It is not a guarantee that these vendors will be able to meet Buy and Build America. … If it’s 100%, we won’t make it. If it’s 70%, maybe? That’s still pretty slim.”

If the vendors cannot comply, the only remaining route to federal money is a Buy America waiver. Blake was candid about those odds as well: “it has been a number of years since FTA has issued a waiver,” and pursuing one “could kick the timeline significantly.” The alternative she named is not a better federal outcome — it is proceeding as a locally funded project.

Vendor procurement is now on hold on the advice of special counsel, pending an answer.

Core Capacity Grant

But SMART faces a bigger problem pursuing an FTA Capital Investment Grant under the Core Capacity category. Congress defined that category narrowly: it funds a substantial investment that increases a corridor's capacity by at least 10 percent in a corridor already at or above capacity.

The federal funding SMART hopes to use is also nearing an expiration date. The IIJA’s Capital Investment Grant funding runs through September 30. Congress recently extended portions of the IIJA, but not the additional CIG funding provided through Division J. Congress can restore it later, but for now, that funding ends this month.

The Mountain Village mayor, Scott Pearson, put the same doubt more plainly: “There are two issues with federal funding,” and the "first is the uncertain nature of it. We’re not even sure whether we qualify for Buy America. There’s also the question of whether we meet their criteria for expanded capacity, because since COVID, gondola ridership has been flat.”

From any realistic perspective, the odds of obtaining FTA funding, even after the current administration, appear to be “unlikely.”

The Consultants Keep Consulting

The 2022 recommendation for a brand-new gondola system came from the consultants, assuming we could obtain federal funding. There was never much discussion of what would happen if we did not get funding to pay for half the project.

That recommendation led to the 3A ballot measure, sold largely on the idea that it would help build a capital fund for a new gondola.

Below is a Livable Telluride chart summarizing funds expended to date on the various consultants, as well as future designated funds.

The combined total is almost $11 million. SMART will also pay almost $5 million of the overall cost, which must come from the same 3A funds touted to build a capital reserve to help build a new gondola. Rather than going toward construction, those funds are going toward more consulting on what we should do about the gondola and how to finance it.

This is where the real problem comes into play. Like other projects I have written about that seem to be on a spinning wheel of consultant activity without sufficient oversight by elected officials, the gondola process appears to be at that point.

The consultants recommended full replacement, and everything since has been built on that recommendation—which was premised on the federal government paying for half of the new system.

So What Do We Do Now?

By the end of this year, roughly $4.4 million will have been spent in consulting fees, all premised on replacing the current system, plus another $6.5 million budgeted for 2027 alone. That spending leads either to a US Department of Transportation application or to figuring out how to pass a ballot measure that everyone will approve to raise about $135 to $187 million, plus station-area costs that have not yet been priced.

One obvious option is to revisit the rejected idea of a major upgrade to the existing system, keeping the gondola stations largely as they are.

The manufacturer said in 2015 that with continued maintenance and capital replacement, it could run indefinitely. 

A good step is that an engineering firm is planning a new engineering study this fall that may provide insights, address needs in the current system, and develop a prioritized risk assessment and recommendations for continued operation over 5- and 10-year planning horizons.  This may help with major upgrade or replacement decisions.

But we, as a community, should have a real discussion about how we ended up in this position after $4.5 million has been spent, based on a concept of federal funding that is either a Hail Mary or a complete pipe dream, and a lot more money to be spent soon.
