Can the Town Simply Ignore Its 
Own Contract?

by Morgan Smith
October 8, 2026

In 1979, the Town of Telluride signed a contract with Telco, the owner of Backman Village land. That contract, the recorded plat, and the covenants set the number of residential units that can be built on the lots now called Carhenge, and the total is 157 plus some commercial. The Town’s proposed design for this property is 220 units. [i]

On September 30, at two hearings, the Town was repeatedly asked a simple question: does the project density comply with the contract the Town signed in 1979? Every official who was asked gave the same answer, which was basically that they were not allowed to discuss it, and there was nobody here to answer that question.

Put those answers together, and a pattern is apparent. The Town knows it has a contract problem. Its own lawyer told it so in writing in 2008. Its strategy is to make sure no one in the development approval process ever addresses that legal question.    

The Town has known for years that owner consent may be required at Backman Village.

This is not a new argument invented by neighbors. The Town has been on notice for almost fifty years: the 1979 Contract makes the plat part of the agreement (§ I(A)) and provides that if Lots 34 and 34B are combined, their densities “shall be added,” not increased (§ III(F)). This means 157 units are allowed and not 220.

The Covenants incorporate the plat’s “densities, standards, and uses” (§ 2). Those limitations are inconsistent with the Town's current development plans for 220 units.

In a 2008 memorandum, Town Attorney Kevin Geiger concluded that because the Backman Village covenants run to the benefit of the owners, “their consent to an amendment to the covenants would appear to be required.”

In the Butcher Creek case, the Town refused to process an application because the developer “had not obtained the consent of all owners within the PUD, as required by the amendments clause.” The Colorado Supreme Court recounted that history in its Kavanaugh decision. [ii]

The Chair 7 Community Coalition raised the issue in numerous letters as early as May and at every hearing since then. As the Coalition’s counsel told the Council, the question was raised “over and over and over” and “met by silence.”

The question that shall not be spoken

At the September 30 Town Council appeal of the Planning and Zoning decision to remove a lot line in Carhenge, Town staff told Town Council they need not, and cannot, consider the fact that Town contractual covenants include restrictions that the Town’s proposed development plans significantly exceed.   

Staff stated that the Town contractual covenants are “a private right that Planning and Zoning and now Town Council and the Town Attorney are not the adjudicators of.” 

Special counsel Gerald Dahl advised the Town Council members: “Do the covenants control the process you use when a plat is amended? They don’t.” Instead, he asserted that the sole issue was compliance with the Telluride Land Use Code, without considering the Town's contractual obligations. 

When asked whether the new plat would contain the same limitations on density and use as the current plat, Dahl told the Town Council, “That’s going to either be or not be on the final plat that staff reviews.” While I love a Shakespeare reference, that hardly answers the question, and it certainly doesn’t make the process feel fair.

So who decides? Dahl answered that, too. It “gets adjudicated by a private right of action that any property owner can bring against any other for violation of the covenants. … So the Town can be a defendant.” In short, sue me or forget it.

That evening, at the HARC meeting to review the large-scale development application for Carhenge, the Assistant Town Attorney Allie Slaten told the commissioners that HARC need not and could not consider the underlying Town’s contractual covenants in its determination.

She asserted that as part of the application process, the Town’s legal department made its own determination on whether the Town had the authority to submit the development application for Carhenge. She explained: “Authority and private covenants are a different issue than mere ownership. Ownership has been shown. The application can continue.” 

So, based on the undisputed fact that the Town owns both lots at Carhenge, the Town decided it had the right to submit its own development application, even though it is not consistent with the Town’s contractual covenants.

During public comment, I asked when the Town would consider the Town contractual covenants in this entire development process; the HARC chair answered: “There’s nobody here to answer that question.” Yes, I am getting the feeling that no one wants to answer this question.

Whatever keeps the project moving

The Town asserts that no commissioner or council member may consider the Town’s own contractual covenants and obligations. Rather, the Town’s process is entirely closed to addressing such a question, leaving only the courthouse, where the neighbors incur all the cost of showing that the Town is not abiding by its own contract.  

This position also conflicts with the Town's actions when it reviewed the proposed Butcher Creek development: it refused to accept the application or allow it to move forward without the express written consent of all current owners, as required by the contract to which the Town was also a party.

Now that the Town owns it, owner consent appears to be someone else’s problem.

In 2008, Town Attorney Geiger determined that current owner consent “would appear to be required” for any amendments to the contractual documents in Backman Village. Now, Dahl says that although he understands why neighbors repeatedly cite this memo, in his view it addressed only a situation in which the Town’s contractual covenants were to be amended. But Dahl’s interpretation misses the obvious point.

The plat’s density limits are written into the covenants and the contract, so by allowing a new Plat that lacks those existing density limitations, the Town is amending the covenants without consent, just not saying so.  

Dahl’s theory also makes no legal sense.  It asserts that since the Town is not amending its contractual requirements according to the process required in the document—but rather simply ignoring them—the 2008 memo is not applicable.

That is not an interpretation. It is a Town-sanctioned breach of contract.

When one council member, Kristin Permakoff, said she believed “the covenants and the plat should run together with the property,” she was told she could vote as she wished, but that she “might make a comment that could void you out of future discussions on Carhenge.” No one in the majority received that warning. The only member cautioned about her comments was the one who said the Town's contract might actually matter and should be considered at some point in this process.

One explanation for this pattern is the Town’s investment to date and a vested interest in moving this project forward. The Town spent more than $2.7 million in consultant fees on Carhenge and Shandoka for plans that greatly exceed the Town’s contracted density and use limits.

Answering “the question” would mean substantially rethinking that work, while not answering it keeps the project moving forward.

Meanwhile, in the Constitution

While the Town is free to develop whatever interpretations of the Telluride Land Use Code it chooses to apply in a given situation—even if they differ entirely from how it handled the issue in prior cases like Butcher Creek—such interpretation does nothing to alter the basic fairness and procedural requirements that exist outside the code.

The Fourteenth Amendment says that no state, including a town, may “deprive any person of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law.” In plain terms, before the government takes something of value from you, it must tell you it intends to do so and give you a fair chance to be heard. 

This is true for a traffic ticket, and it’s certainly true for contractual obligations long recognized as a property right. As the U.S. Supreme Court put it, “valid contracts are property, whether the obligor be a private individual, a municipality, a State, or the United States.” Lynch v. United States, 292 U.S. 571, 579 (1934).

The Fifth Amendment provides that the government may not take property for public use without just compensation. The Supreme Court held in Knick v. Township of Scott (2019) that an owner’s claim arises “at the time of the taking.” A government that takes without paying has already violated the Constitution. The lawful route is to condemn and pay, not to take and wait to be sued.

The Town’s answer, “you can always sue,” turns that order upside down.

It shifts the cost, delay, and burden to the people the contract is meant to protect. Additionally, neighbors face even higher hurdles to enforce the Town’s contractual obligations. Under a 2024 state law that is likely unconstitutional, a neighbor who loses a challenge to a housing approval must pay the Town’s legal fees in addition to their own.

This means the neighborhood group must not only fund its own attorney to challenge the law, but if the lawsuit is unsuccessful, it must also pay the Town's attorney fees. Who in their right mind would take that chance, which is exactly what the Town seems to assume.

What the Town can still do

None of this is inevitable. Before the final development approvals are given, the Town can:

1.     Obtain and publish a written legal determination from independent counsel on whether the 1979 contract allows 220 units without amending the original documents, or

2.    Redesign Carhenge to the 157 units the Town contractual covenants allow.

3.    If the Town believes that the contractual limits are valid, but must give way to the larger development, then it must file a condemnation action to remove those limitations.

Any of the above provides the owners with a hearing, but the Town’s current approach only gives them an expensive lawsuit. 

What’s next: HARC extended all three Carhenge design applications through December 16, 2026. Written comments should be sent to townclerk@telluride.gov. For background, see last week’s newsletter. For full disclosure, the author is assisting the legal challenge by the Chair 7 Community Coalition. 

[i] Technically, the applicant is Design Workshop, hired by the Town to apply on its behalf.

[ii] Kavanaugh v. Telluride Locals Coalition Petitioners’ Committee ¶ 5 (Colo. 2026). If the Town had the authority to reject the application for lack of consent, the Town clearly felt comfortable reaching the underlying merits of the contract it signed with the developer in the form of a PUD Agreement.
